# The third chair: eight Draw Days in a life born outside

*Narrative sketch from a working session, 10 Nov 2026, rewritten the same day for the fitting. One life lived outside a household's allowance, in Tansy, a drawn-holdings drum one glide from Sorrel on Herb Row, told on eight Draw Days between the ages of eight and a hundred and twelve. Builds on families.md (born to a place, the fitting, leave, the draw, the placeless-born, the lot, waiting wallets, drums that chose otherwise), people.md (places and shares, the placeless, houses), economy.md (the dividend, the price of an hour, rent), eating-out.md (Tansy, Strain & Vintage), ashgrove.md (Herb Row, the Eleven Days) and raft-habitat.md (the drum). Numbers come from those docs and from `models/placeless_share.py`. Jeff's steer of 10 Nov 2026: the sketch is set in Tansy; standing is settled by birth and travels by certificate; and, later the same day, the fitting is the default, with a few drums choosing otherwise. The first version of this sketch was written before that decision, for a drum that fitted nobody, and what it found about such drums is now in families.md § Free drums. Everything with a name is invented, and where the docs leave a choice open the sketch picks one; both are listed under What the sketch assumes. Speech and names are given in translation.*

## At a glance
- **Where:** Tansy, a steady drum of 200 million shares on Herb Row of Merrow (eating-out.md), and in it Hartsease, a block of about 3,000 on decks 52–57, at 0.93 g.
- **Who:** Nell Varga, the third child of Dov and Marguerite Varga, born outside their allowance. Her brother Tomas is seven years older and her sister Liesl four, and both were born to a place. Jonah Abara holds a place on deck 148. Bas Lindqvist and Odalys Ferro were born outside too.
- **What she is:** placeless-born (families.md). Tansy says *born outside*, and calls such a child a third chair. She is registered, fed, schooled and fitted like her brother and sister, and from eighteen she holds a wallet like theirs. She can't inherit a place, be named heir to one or bring children into one unless the lot places her, and until it does her implant stays in.
- **The days:** Draw Day at eight, fourteen, eighteen, twenty-nine, thirty-one, sixty-four, eighty-one and a hundred and twelve. The last is the present.
- **How common she is:** about one birth in 400 in Tansy is outside the allowance, ~4,200 a year, and one in seventeen of those children is ever placed. Nell is one of the sixteen.
- **New here:** how Tansy draws and places; a clinic's mistake; the fitting and having them out; a waiting wallet and how it moves; a free drum seen from a fitted one; marrying late.

## Tansy
- **A drawn-holdings drum** (families.md § Kinds of drum). Places pass whole to one heir. A household has two children by right and a third by leave. The heirless name an heir for their place, and the rest of the drum's room is drawn by lot.
- **Fitted,** like nearly every drum on the Row. Everyone has a contraceptive implant from fourteen. A couple with a place has theirs out for the asking, for two children, and again on a permit for a third.
- **Draw Day** falls a week after Booking Day, when the year's room has been counted. About 43,600 permits are drawn at the nine station rings, some 4,850 at each, and every one is read out with the name of a household that ended without an heir. At five seconds a name it takes about seven hours. A couple with two children and none outside has about one chance in 450 each year, and one in eighteen over the twenty-five years it can enter.
- **The placings** are read last. Everyone born outside and not yet placed is entered from eighteen to sixty, and Tansy draws about 250 a year, twenty-eight at each ring: one chance in about 680 a year, and one in seventeen over a life. It is the only door.
- **The keepers of the draw** are seven people from other drums of the Row, sworn for seven years and lodged at the drum's cost. Tansy's own offices of trust are full of people with no heirs, and those people have nieces and nephews.
- **Waiting wallets.** About six million of Tansy's shares are unheld on any day, each waiting in a family for its next child. Tansy lends the dividend of one to each of its people born outside, from eighteen, for life.
- **Its history.** Tansy fitted nobody until its last Count, about six hundred years ago. Before that it was a free drum: one person in forty born outside and shareless, a keep posted at every ring, and houses on one place in fifty, which it once emptied (the Emptying, about nine hundred years ago) and spent seven centuries refounding. The old Count board hangs in the Register hall. The motion to take the implants out again comes to the ballot about once a generation.

## One: eight
*Nell is eight. Tomas is fifteen and Liesl twelve.*

Tansy's schools take every class of eight-year-olds to hear the draw, the way Sorrel's take theirs to the Window. Nell's class stands at the rail of Station Ring 5 from the second hour, above the Quaysides, where the platform has been cleared and a keeper from Yarrow reads from a lectern in a flat, careful voice.

"Permit three thousand and six. In the name of the household of Ines and Corwin Maddox, deck two hundred and one, which ended. Drawn for the household of Sunniva and Joss Pell, deck sixty-four."

Somewhere down the platform people shout, and a couple is lifted off their feet. A third by leave is congratulated like a wedding, and the Pells will very likely name the child Ines or Corwin. Nell's class has been told to count how many of the day's winners they know. By the fourth hour her deskmate Pia has one, because the keeper reads out Pia's own parents, and Pia is sick with joy over the rail.

There are thirty-one children in the class. Most classes in Tansy have nobody whose parents are out of the draw. This one has two, Nell and a boy called Bas Lindqvist, and they have both noticed.

At dinner she asks why the Vargas never go.

"We had our third," says her mother. Marguerite is forty-seven and tired, and she says it kindly. "You."

Liesl explains it later, in the room they share, with the family's page of the Register on her slate. When Liesl was born the clinic on Ring 5 put Dov's and Marguerite's implants back, as it does after a second child. That season it put them back in ninety couples from a batch that had been stored warm and was dead. Nobody knew until the pregnancies started. The clinic recalled every couple it could find, and most of those who were already carrying ended it. Eleven didn't.

"They knew what you'd be," Liesl says. "They had about a week to decide."

The page has three lines for the children. Tomas: born to a place. Liesl: born to a place. Nell: a dash. Under them is the note that the household is out of the draw. An allowance is a number, and the Register doesn't record whose fault a third was. If it did, every clinician with a kind heart would find a dead batch for somebody.

The fault was the drum's, so Tansy pays the eleven households a child's wallet until each of those children is eighteen. The Vargas aren't short. Bas's family isn't either, and for the same reason. Three decks up there is a family that cut after nineteen years in the draw, and they are five people on four wallets, 17.3 kays a head a day where Pia's family has 21.6. Hartsease says of them that they are *carrying a chair*. It says of the Vargas that they were handed one.

## Two: fourteen
*Nell is fourteen. Liesl is eighteen and Tomas twenty-one.*

In Tansy the fourteen-year-olds are fitted in Draw Day week, a class at a time. Nell's class walks up to the clinic on deck 54 in a line that keeps breaking into pairs. It takes four minutes each. The clinician finds the place on the inside of the upper arm, numbs it, and slides the rod in under the skin. Then she checks it twice with her fingers and once with a reader, and writes the batch number on a card the family keeps. That has been the rule at every clinic in Tansy for fourteen years.

"You're a Varga," the clinician says, reading the card back. She is old enough to have been there.

"Yes."

"I'll check yours three times."

There is a dinner afterwards, as there is in every flat on the deck that has a fourteen-year-old. Dov has bought a shell egg. Tomas is home from the vat lines. Somebody always asks the fourteen-year-old the same question, and this time it is Liesl, who got her dividend last month and feels like the adult at the table.

"So when does it come out?"

It is supposed to be a joke about weddings. Liesl hears what she has said about half a second after she says it.

"When I'm drawn," Nell says. "It's a draw. Somebody gets drawn."

"One in seventeen," says Tomas, who has looked it up.

Marguerite cuts the egg into five. All day Nell has been exactly like the other thirty children in her class, down to the plaster on her arm, and she will remember it as the last day that was true.

## Three: eighteen
*Nell is eighteen.*

On the morning she comes of age the drum's wallet stops coming to the household and a wallet starts coming to her. Tomas's dividend went into his own name at eighteen, and Liesl's: 21.6 kays a day each, for life, for nothing, on a share that came down the family from somebody dead whose name the Register can give. Nell's is the same 21.6 kays. The Ledger shows where it comes from: a share of a family called Okafor, on deck 210, whose holder died last spring and whose child hasn't been born yet. When that child is born the share is the child's, and Nell's wallet will move under her to another share that is waiting.

"It's a real wallet," Dov says. He has checked the Ledger four times.

"It is," she says. "It just isn't anybody's."

She has taken a post as a vault hand at the strain library, which is the longest on the Row and the thing Tansy is proudest of after its vat lines. It pays what any full week of work pays, about 17 kays a day, so she has 38.6, the same as Tomas.

On Draw Day she and Bas stand at the rail of Ring 5 for the placings, which come at the end of the seventh hour, after every third has been read. It is the first year either of them is entered. The keeper reads twenty-eight names, each in the name of a household that ended, and the platform claps for each one, a little less than it clapped for the thirds.

"Forty-one more of those," says Bas, who can subtract eighteen from sixty.

## Four: twenty-nine
*Nell is twenty-nine. Jonah Abara is thirty-seven.*

She met Jonah when she was twenty-six, over a contaminated flask in the receiving room. He is a steward on the vat lines and holds his great-grandmother's place on deck 148: an heir, unmarried at thirty-four, which in Tansy makes a man interesting. They married when she was twenty-seven. A marriage across the line is a marriage. It brings no children into the place until the one from outside is placed, so his implant has stayed in beside hers, in a flat with three bedrooms.

This is their third Draw Day at the rail. It is well into the eighth hour when the keeper from Yarrow reaches the placings.

Hers isn't read.

The fourteenth name is Odalys Ferro, who trained Nell in the vaults. Odalys was born outside too, to parents who cut, and she has been in the lot for sixteen years. She is thirty-four. She is read in the name of a household on deck 233 that ended, and she will have its place, and by the end of the week she will have had her implant out. Nell holds her while she cries.

Jonah does the sum on the cab home, because he is a steward and sums are how he is kind. One chance in 680 a year. She has thirty-one entries left, and all of them together come to about one in twenty-two. The clinic has banked what they'd need, so a win at fifty-nine would still be two children. Twenty-one times in twenty-two it is no children at all.

"I can do thirty years of anything with you," he says. "I don't think I can do that."

She doesn't argue. A man who marries her and waits gives up two children he could have placed, for one chance in twenty-two of getting them back. For three million generations the people who took Jonah's bet left nobody, and she thinks that most of what he calls his feelings is that arithmetic.

They part before the next Booking Day. He marries a woman called Ilse the year after. Nell hears from a vault hand on deck 148 the week they have theirs out, and she sees the birth read on the deck's board when she is thirty-three, and another when she is thirty-six.

## Five: thirty-one
*Nell is thirty-one.*

She spent most of a year away first. Mugwort is three rows over, a free drum, one of the few in the metro that fits nobody. Half the Row disapproves of it and knows the way there.

Tansy wrote her a certificate, which says that Tansy answers for her commons wherever she lodges and for any child she has there. Mugwort took her in on it without a question. The clinic on its Ring 3 has a queue in Draw Day week, couples from Tansy and Sorrel and Lovage who weren't read. It will take out anybody's implant in a minute and ask nothing. She walked past it most days.

Mugwort has ten times Tansy's third chairs, about one person in forty, and it can't lend that many a wallet. They work for 17 kays a day and no dividend. A keep is posted at every ring, 13.2 kays a day, for a household that has a child outside. The old ones live on what a house's box pays, about four kays a day, if a house elected them. Down by Ring 7 there is a quarter where the people born outside have had children, who were born outside, and had children. Mugwort's assembly talks about that quarter the way Tansy's talks about blight.

Her landlady there had two children born outside and a third on the way.

"You could have yours out tomorrow," the landlady said. "Nobody here would look twice."

"It would be Tansy's child," Nell said. "On my certificate. Born outside, with a mother who'd just put herself out of the lot." She had done that sum on the glide over, and she did it again most nights.

So she is at the rail of Ring 5 again with her implant in. The motion to take the implants out of Tansy was on the Booking Day ballot last week, as it is about once a generation, and so was the motion for a third by right. Every couple on this platform has thought about both. The first lost by eight points and the second by three.

Her name isn't read. At the end of the day the houses read their elections. Library House holds forty places on decks 30 and 31, above the vaults, and ninety keepers, and it elects two this year. Nell is the second. A house isn't what it was in the old Tansy, the last door for people with no wallet. Most of the ninety were born to a place and chose a hall over a household. What she gets is a room of 24 m², a hall where a person cooks, and eighty-nine people who will notice if she doesn't come down to dinner.

## Six: sixty-four
*Nell is sixty-four. Jonah is seventy-two.*

In between: she was thirty-eight in the Eleven Days, and spent them in the vault corridor with a hand lamp, logging the cold by hand. She was forty-seven when a blight took Tansy's *Fusarium* line in nine days. The vaults hold about two hundred thousand strains, and the one she brought up, a *Neurospora* that the keepers before her had carried for four hundred years without once needing it, fed two hundred million people for a season. The steaks came out nutty and a little orange. Tansy's blight years are vintages (eating-out.md), and the library put the keeper's name on the line, as it does.

Bas was drawn at forty-four. He had stopped coming to the rail years before, and heard it from a neighbour. He married at forty-six and had two children by fifty-one. Odalys has grandchildren.

Nell's last entry was five years ago. The keeper read twenty-eight names, and the next week she went up to the clinic on deck 54 and had the rod taken out, since nothing rode on it any more. Some people keep theirs in. She has a white line on the inside of her arm the length of a matchstick, and she finds she doesn't mind it.

Jonah finds her at Strain & Vintage on Draw Day, at the long counter on the Ring 5 Quaysides, because it is the one day she is sure to be off shift and he knows where a keeper would eat. He and Ilse parted two years ago, when their younger child had her place, as a good many couples do with ninety years still ahead of them. Both children are householders. His line is set, and nothing he does now can cost it anything.

He orders the flight and pushes the middle plate across to her.

"I have eaten that," she says. "I ate it for five months."

Tansy calls it marrying late. Nothing rides on the lot and nobody reads it from a lectern. She moves into the place on deck 148 the week after Knotnight, into rooms she last slept in at twenty-nine. The House keeps her seat, since election is for life.

## Seven: eighty-one
*Nell is eighty-one. Tomas is eighty-eight and Liesl eighty-five.*

Dov died last year at a hundred and twenty, and Marguerite this spring, at the same age, in the flat in Hartsease where all three of their children were born.

The clerk of the Register read it out in the block's hall at Hartsease, as the clerk does, with the family standing. Dov's share had gone the year before to a great-great-grandson. Marguerite's goes to Ida, who is three, Liesl's great-granddaughter, born to a place and shareless since birth. The place goes down its own line to the eldest descendant of household age without a household, who is Petra, twenty-four, Tomas's granddaughter.

Nell stands between her brother and her sister and listens. The clerk doesn't pause at her name, because nothing on his page was ever going to be hers. Her wallet has never been a Varga share. In sixty-three years it has rested on eleven other families' shares, the Okafors' first, and each time a child was born the Ledger sent her one line to say where it rested now.

On Draw Day she goes down to Hartsease to help Petra move in. Her old room has been let to lodgers for fifty years and smells of somebody else's soap. The table is the same table. It has two chairs on the long side and a third at the end, which doesn't match, because Dov bought it second-hand in the week they had to decide.

"Which was yours?" Petra asks, and then hears herself.

"That one," says Nell. "Keep it. You'll want it if you're drawn."

Petra is twenty-four and has just been handed seventy-eight square metres and the right to two children. She and her intended are having theirs out on Thursday, and she says, very seriously, that after the second they'll have the new rods read every year, both of them, at a different clinic. Tomas, who is eighty-eight and was seven the week his parents decided, looks at the third chair and doesn't say anything.

## Eight: a hundred and twelve
*Nell is a hundred and twelve. This is the present.*

Jonah died last winter at a hundred and nineteen, after forty-seven years of marrying late. His place went down his line. His children are seventy-eight and seventy-five and have had households for half a century, and his grandchildren have theirs, so the eldest descendant without one was a great-grandson, Emeka, who is fourteen and was fitted this year. The place is his, held in trust by his mother until he is of age, with its rooms let for him meanwhile.

Emeka's mother came to see Nell the week after the reading. She was kind about it and had plainly been dreading it. Tansy gives a widow from across the line a year's grace in the place. After that Nell could stay as long as she liked, as a lodger, in the back room, at the going rate, which a trust is bound to charge, because a trust can't give away a child's rents. Twenty square metres is two kays a day. She has 21.6. It was never the money.

She was back in Library House before the year was out. Her seat had been kept for forty-eight years, and her room had had eleven keepers in it since. The House eats together, and the hall has a person cooking. She has a small lamp over a shelf of herbs, and more worlds than she has evenings.

On Draw Day the House's two newest keepers take her down to the rail of Ring 5, because she asked, and because nobody at Library House has heard as many draws. A keeper from Borage reads this year. Four thousand eight hundred and fifty permits, each in the name of a household that ended, and twenty-eight placings after them. She has worked out, over a hundred and four years of these, what it is that she won't leave. No permit will be read in her name, because she heads no household to end. No child will be named for her, because children are named for the dead whose shares they carry, and what she has carried for ninety-four years was never a share. When she dies it stops, and the share under it goes on waiting for its child.

In the sixth hour the keeper reads a permit in the name of a household on deck 301 that ended, drawn for the household of Petra and Mattis Brandt, Hartsease, deck fifty-five. Petra is fifty-five and has entered for twenty-one years. "She kept the chair," Nell says, out loud, to nobody, and the two young keepers look at each other.

Afterwards they take her along the Quaysides to Strain & Vintage and she lets them order. The list is the length of a wine cellar's. Two-thirds of the way down, between a mild *Fusarium* from a good year and a koji-aged cut with a crust like old cheese, is line 61,204 of the strain library, sixty-five years in the vault since the season it fed the drum, and still listed the way the library wrote it. People who order it mostly think Varga is a place.

## What the sketch assumes
**Choices among options the docs leave open:**
- **Tansy's kind.** A drawn-holdings drum (families.md), fitted, with the implants out for a place, the forfeit of the draw, no keep, and about one in seventeen of its placeless-born placed, by the lot alone.
- **The lot's years.** Entered from eighteen to sixty, and the clinic takes the implant out afterwards for anyone who asks.
- **Draw Day's order:** thirds, then placings, then the houses' elections. Tansy's schools take eight-year-olds to hear it, and its clinics fit the fourteen-year-olds the same week.
- **Parting.** With ninety years of adult life, many couples part once their children have households. The place stays with whoever holds it.
- **The Register's clerk reads a share and a place** to the family at a death, as Sorrel's does at the Down Halls (sorrel.md).

**New here (proposals):**
- **A clinic's mistake is the household's child.** A child born after a dead batch is placeless-born and its household is out of the draw, exactly as after a cut, because an allowance that forgave mistakes would find them made on purpose. The drum pays in kWh what it can't mend in standing: a child's wallet to the household until the child is eighteen. Since the batch, Tansy's clinics read every implant after fitting and write its batch on a card.
- **A waiting wallet moves.** It rests on one unheld share until that share's child is born, about six years on average, and then on another. The Ledger tells the holder each time.
- **A placing comes with a place.** It is read in the name of a household that ended, and someone drawn who has no place to marry into is given that household's.
- **Marriage across the line waits on the lot.** The partner born outside has their own entries and the marriage adds none.
- **Marrying late.** Most marriages between the placeless-born and the born to a place are second marriages, after the partner's children have households, when they cost a line nothing. A widow or widower from across the line has a year's grace in the place and no claim on it.
- **Houses in a fitted drum** belong mostly to people who chose them. Election is for life.
- **Tansy's history.** A free drum until its last Count about six hundred years ago, with the Emptying of its houses behind it.
- **Mugwort,** a free drum three rows over, with a quarter by its Ring 7 where the line outside has taken.
- **The certificate.** Standing is settled by birth. A drum writes a certificate for one of its people who lodges elsewhere, answering for their commons and for any child born to them there.
- **Keepers from outside.** Tansy hires the keepers of its draw from other drums for fixed terms.
- **Smaller things.** "Born outside"; "third chair", "carrying a chair" and "handed one"; "having them out"; a strain listed under its keeper's name.

## Questions it raises
The questions about rules (whether the lot should be the only door, what a trust owes a widow from across the line, what a certificate is worth to a host) are in the open questions of families.md. The sketch's own:

- **The week to decide.** Dov and Marguerite could have ended the pregnancy and stayed in the draw. Whether a drum should ask that of a couple it failed, and whether any other rule keeps mercy from becoming the leak.
- **The other sixteen.** Nell is elected to a house, marries late and is never drawn. What the same life looks like for a third chair whose parents cut, in a block that knows it. (deliberately open)
- **The quarter by Ring 7.** What Mugwort does when its line outside is a tenth of its births. (deliberately open)

## Method
- **Sums:** `models/placeless_share.py`, output in `models/out/placeless_share.txt`.
- **Hartsease:** decks 52–57, r = 3,000 − 4(n − 1) = 2,796–2,776 m, 0.93 g. Jonah's place on deck 148: 2,412 m, 0.80 g. Library House on decks 30–31: 0.96 g.
- **How many:** births outside the allowance are one in 400 of ~1.67 M a year, ~4,200; one in seventeen is placed, ~250 a year, 28 a ring. Carried, ~470,000: one person in 430, seven in a block of 3,000.
- **The batch:** a ring clinic serving ~30 blocks sees ~360 second births a year, so ninety couples is a season's refitting. Two of the eleven children in one class of 31 is the cluster a batch makes; by chance alone a class has a third chair one time in thirteen.
- **Wallets:** a family of four has 4 × 21.6 kWh; five people on four wallets have 17.3 kWh a head. Full time is 3.1 kWh × 2,000 h ÷ 365 ≈ 17 kWh a day (economy.md); with a wallet, 38.6.
- **The draw:** room is 5.7% of ~1.67 M births, ~94,400 slots a year, half the households' to name and half the drum's. Less ~3,300 second twins, which Tansy charges to its room (families.md § Twins), and 250 placings, ~43,600 permits are drawn, ~4,850 a station ring; with the placings, ~6.8 hours at five seconds each. A household's chance by the draw alone is 5.5% (one in eighteen) over ~25 years, ~0.22% a year (one in 450).
- **The lot:** 6% over 42 entries is 1 − 0.94^(1/42) ≈ one in 680 a year. With 31 entries left, 1 − (1 − 1 ÷ 680)³¹ ≈ 4.5%, one in twenty-two.
- **The waiting wallet:** ~6 M unheld shares × 0.9 kW ≈ 5.4 GW; ~400,000 placeless-born adults take 0.36 GW. A share waits about as long for a child as a child waits for a share, ~6 years (people.md), so a wallet moves about every six years: eleven times by eighty-one.
- **Mugwort:** families.md's free drum. Three births in a hundred outside, one in eight placed, 2.6% over par, about one person in forty born outside; a keep of half the child's commons, 0.55 kW = 13.2 kWh a day; a box paying ~3.9 kWh a day.
- **Rent:** 20 m² × 0.1 kWh per m² a day (economy.md) = 2 kWh a day.
- **Ages:** Marguerite was 39 and Dov 40 at Nell's birth, so they die at 120 when she is 81 and 80. Tomas was born when Dov was 33; his eldest child at 32 and that child's eldest at 32 make Petra 24 when Nell is 81. Jonah is eight years older than Nell; his children were born when he was 41 and 44, so at his death at 119 they are 78 and 75, his grandchildren in their forties, and his eldest great-grandchild about 14: one of the places in seven that people.md's family-tree model sends to a child.
- **The present:** Nell's eighth Draw Day here. The Eleven Days were 74 years ago (ashgrove.md), when she was 38, and the *Neurospora* year 65 years ago, when she was 47.
