## Summary
- **Chance does almost nothing; selection and medicine do everything.** At ~10²⁰ people a raft, a neutral variant would take ~10²⁰ generations to spread by luck. Rafts in the same conditions evolve almost identically.
- **Fixed families switch off most natural selection.** With births held to deaths, nearly every adult who reaches parenthood has about the same number of children. Unchecked, each child's ~1–2 new harmful mutations would pile up, and health would fall ~1% a generation.
- **Proofreading clears them.** The norm everywhere: every embryo is sequenced and its new mutations are put back to match its parents, along with known disease variants. It's a norm, not a law, and some drums refuse it.
- **So people look like all of humanity mixed together, for as long as the civilization lasts.** The average is medium brown, every old feature recombined, the range as wide as Earth's. Nobody tans, jaws are small, bones are light and nearly everyone wears corrected eyes. Any emigrant can marry anyone, anywhere in the Shade.
- **Places are household land, passed whole to one heir; shares are people's.** A nation drum of 200 M has ~90 M places, one to a household, like farms. Children and young adults live in a parent's or grandparent's place. An heir usually inherits a great-grandparent's place in their late 20s, and a spouse marries in. Each person's wallet comes with a share, freed by a death in the family and passed to a newborn. A child born ahead of a death has no share until one frees, so families that have extra children live thinner.
- **Children belong to a place, and are born to one.** Couples have them once they have a household, and a household has two by right and a third by leave. A child born outside that allowance is placeless-born: fed, schooled and proofread like any other, with no claim on a place unless it is placed. So a drum has only as many households with children as it has places, and its extra children become the placeless, not more households.
- **The placeless have full lives without children of their own.** They marry, live in houses held by bodies that don't die, foster and teach, and keep the drum's offices of trust. In a steady drum they are about one person in forty, nearly all of them placeless-born.
- **The wish for children is saturated, and standing holds the numbers.** About one couple in twenty has no children, so the families with a third replace them and gain by at least the room they leave. Nothing a drum can live with removes that, so within a few thousand years of any start most couples want a third, and the Shade is far older. What holds a drum is the allowance. Leave for a third is issued only as fast as the childless leave room, to about one family in eight, by lot, gift, sale or a block's say, and a child born outside it can't inherit. A household that waits keeps its chance of leave and one that doesn't gives it up, so the rule holds wherever fewer of the placeless-born are placed than households are given thirds (What births do to the gene pool; families.md).
- **Drums choose their own birth systems.** Each gets a fixed allotment of beam, so a drum that overbreeds hurts only itself. Most run on inheritance and the allowance, with a draw for thirds and a posted price for the keep of a child born outside it; open drums leave the giving to their blocks; some keep a pool with formal birth rights (Sorrel and Lovage in ashgrove.md); about 1% coerce.
- **Counted drums license births and enforce it.** They are drums that had to shrink, which standing can't do. Most fit a contraceptive implant at fourteen and stop births for a decade or two; the darkest are rare and shunned. Whether people can glide out decides how dark they get.
- **Open drums are the bright side.** About a quarter of drums hold births to deaths freely, keep a small Margin under par and open their rings both ways (open-drums.md).
- **The clinics see nearly every birth.** Children born outside them, the wild-born, are unproofread and often shareless.

## Who breeds with whom
| Level | People | Gene flow | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nation drum | ~2 × 10⁸ | Most partners come from inside the drum | Fully mixed within a few dozen generations |
| Raft | ~6 × 10¹⁹ (0.1 AU) to ~1.5 × 10²² (1.6 AU cap), ~600,000 to ~8 million drums across | Glides reach the next drums free; cyclers make far moves cheap but slow | Without proofreading, regional looks could last for millions of years if few people move far |
| Shade | ~10³³ in 10¹¹–10¹³ rafts | Almost none: rafts pass at 100–300 km/s, and emigrants come only as swaps | Each raft is a gene pool on its own. Medical standards travel by laser, like recipes |

- **Every possible single-letter mutation turns up in each drum about once a generation.** Nothing waits on luck; whatever is selected for spreads.
- **Rafts start alike.** A raft founded by whole drums carries almost all its parent's variation, so founder effects are negligible.

## What the drum does to a body
Most of what the drum changes is what it stops asking for.

| Factor | Within a lifetime | Over generations, without proofreading |
|---|---|---|
| Gravity: children raised at 0.85–0.98 g, most adults at 0.6–0.85 g (raft-habitat.md) | Lighter bones, less muscle, slightly longer spines | Tolerance of 1 g slowly erodes |
| Light: lamps with no UV, most hours indoors | Nobody tans; near-universal myopia from short sightlines | Skin colour is free of selection and follows taste |
| Air: heliox in warm rooms, oxygen held at 21 kPa around the clock (the food plant never pauses; cuisine.md's ~16–17 kPa was the end of a 40–55-day layer-era eclipse) | Helium chills skin and brightens voices (habitat-design.md) | Nothing: oxygen handling goes unselected |
| Diet: complete, soft, synthetic (food-system.md) | Small jaws, crowded teeth | Unused: starvation resistance, bitter taste, digestive specialisation |
| Disease: dense drums, ~45% of residents glide out and back daily | One virus reaches 200 M people fast | Immune genes keep the strongest selection; defences against extinct Earth diseases fade |
| Radiation: ~1 mSv/yr, mostly cosmic rays (habitat-design.md) | ~0.12 Sv over a 120-year life: ~0.7% more cancer | ~30 mSv a generation: ~3% more new mutations, about what a father adds by waiting a year |

## Proofreading
- **What it is.** Every embryo, or the gametes before it, is sequenced and compared with both parents. Each new mutation (~70 a child) is put back, and known disease variants are repaired. Drums can also bank young gametes, so older parents pass on fewer new mutations.
- **What it does.** It clears mutation load completely without selection, and it stops new variation. The gene pool becomes a fixed, endlessly reshuffled sample of founding humanity, too big for chance to wear down. Mate choice and uneven family sizes can still shift common variants. Every variant stays one the founders carried, but over millions of generations selection can fix many small ones together and carry a trait well past anything in the founders (What births do to the gene pool).
- **The line creeps.** What counts as disease is a choice made millions of times: myopia, crooked teeth, baldness, allergies, short stature. Over ~1 Myr most drums drift toward a wider definition, and people end up a little healthier-looking and a little more alike. Some drums hold the line firmly.
- **One species.** With nothing new added, every raft keeps the same genes, so the Shade stays one interfertile species for its whole ~2 Gyr.
- **Refusers.** Drums or families that refuse proofreading on principle accumulate load. They need some other way to clear it: choosing among embryos, letting thriving families have more children, or slow decline and growing dependence on medicine. Over ~100 Myr an isolated refuser raft would become a separate species.
- **Enhancers.** Drums that edit freely are possible but outside the norm. Their designs travel by laser like any information, so a fashion can cross the Shade in one lifetime. Bodies tuned to the 2 kWe life would be smaller, with eyes that see by a tenth the light, and the question of whether a smaller person counts as a whole share becomes constitutional.

## Births
### What binds
- **Each drum gets a fixed allotment of beam** from its raft and decides what to do with it (governance.md). Extra people don't take beam from neighbours; they thin their own drum's commons and their families' wallets.
- **Metal is fixed.** New metal founds new drums at the raft's edge, ~400 a year on a 0.1 AU raft (economy.md); per existing drum that is one share every ~3.6 years (and a place every ~8), against ~1.7 M births a year at 120-year lives, so over a generation births match deaths or thin the drum.
- **A drum with no limit hits the wall in about a century.** At three children a couple it is 10% over par in ~25 years, down to the lean 1 kWe a person in ~95, and at the bottom of the survival floor (~0.5 kWe, 25 m² of floor) in ~155. Growth starts slowly, at ~0.4% a year, because at first only the newest cohort is bigger. It reaches its long-run ~1.2% a year after about a century. Every lasting drum has some way of holding births to deaths, formal or not. The commonest is that children belong to a place, and are born to one (Places and shares).

### Places and shares
The common system, and closest to how most land-limited societies on Earth worked: they limited who could set up a household rather than who could give birth. Where inheritance was impartible, a farm passed whole to one heir, and the other children married onto a farm, found another living or stayed single: Japan's *ie*, the Alpine farm, Ireland's after the Famine, Central Europe's *Anerbenrecht*. Drums do the same. A drum keeps two ledgers (economy.md).

- **A place is household land, passed whole to one heir.** A nation drum of 200 M people is divided into ~90 M places, each ~78 m² of home, about 2¼ people's share. A place is one household's: a couple, their children and anyone they take in. Children and young adults live in a parent's or grandparent's place. When a place's last holder dies it passes to one heir, so nobody is turned out when the householders die, and the heir's spouse marries in.
- **A place goes down its own line.** A place frees when its holder dies at ~120 and lands about three generations down: the eldest great-grandchild without a household, or whoever the holders named. Failing anyone of household age in the holder's line, it goes to a niece's or nephew's line, then to a child held in trust, then to an adopted heir. It never goes up a generation to an older relative who has waited longer, and that's what makes the system hold (see below). A family-tree model of a drum with stem places sends three places in five down the holder's own line, almost all to great-grandchildren, three in ten to a niece's or nephew's line and one in ten to an adopted heir. About one in seven goes to a child.
- **Most people head a household in their late 20s.** Adult heirs inherit at ~27 on average (10–90%: 21–32), often a year or two before they marry, and let rooms or house a sibling meanwhile. A spouse joins at the wedding, at ~28–29. About three weddings in four have a place already; the rest wait a median of ~3 years, and one in four of them is still waiting at five. Younger adults live in the family's place or lodge in others': the drum's version of Europe's servants in husbandry.
- **Children belong to a place.** Couples have their children once they have a household, so first children come at ~32. It's the rule modern couples keep when they wait for a home of their own before having children, and the Shade's people feel it as a wish, not a law (What births do to the gene pool).
- **Born to a place (proposal).** A household's allowance is two children by right and a third by leave. A child born inside it is born to a place: it can inherit one, be named heir to one or marry into one, and the clinic records it at the birth. A child born outside it is placeless-born: registered, proofread, fed and schooled like its brother and sister, and passed over by the line rule unless it is placed, by adoption into an heirless place, by a marriage the drum allows, or by long service. Earth's land-short societies did the same. They never limited births; they limited heirs (families.md § Born to a place).
- **A share is a person's wallet.** Every person at par holds one: the dividend (21.6 kWh a day) on top of the commons that everyone gets, shared or not (economy.md). A death frees a share. It goes first to the family's longest-waiting shareless member, if there is one, and otherwise to the next child born in the family, who is often named for the dead.
- **Most children start shareless.** About three babies in five are born before the death in the family that frees their share. They wait for it ~6 years on average, and one in ten waits 12 years or more. So at par ~3% of people are shareless, nearly all of them children on their families' wallets. Among people born to a place, shareless adults are rare. The placeless-born stand last in their families' lines for a share in most drums, and they are most of a steady drum's shareless adults.
- **One death, two inheritances.** A couple's 16 great-grandparents die around the couple's 20s and 30s, and on average they free one place for the couple, which one partner inherits, and a share for each of two children. A couple's last death gives a home to a great-grandchild, and each death a wallet, most often to a great-great-grandchild. Births follow places, and wallets follow deaths, inside each family.
- **A family can overspend.** A child born ahead of a death has no share until one frees in the family, so the household divides its wallets: a third child takes a family of four from 21.6 kWh a head to ~17. The child still eats at the Mill table, because the commons is per head. So an extra child's wallet half (0.9 kW) falls on the family and its commons half (1.1 kW) on the whole drum, which is why most drums charge a keep for a child born outside the allowance (economy.md § Children), and why no charge ever held a drum's numbers: what holds them is the child's standing (What births do to the gene pool). Overspent households have less floor and fewer kWh each, and suffer first in a Dark, like Ireland's subdivided plots.
- **The shareless work.** An adult without a share gets the commons but no dividend, and lives on family wallets and work: at ~3 kWh an hour, full-time work earns ~17 kWh a day, ~80% of a wallet (economy.md). A share is a cushion and the freedom not to work, not survival. Most of the shareless get one in time, as deaths in the family free shares.
- **Childlessness is rare,** about one adult in twenty: householders with no child to be their heir, and the placeless. So two children is normal and a third is notable. At ~5% childless, ~12% of families have a third child, which replaces the childless and the few who die before 35. Each further point of childlessness makes room for about two more families in a hundred to have a third, so a third in 15% of families needs ~6% childless. At par that balance is exact: the families with thirds fill the room the childless leave, which is why wanting a third always paid a little (families.md § The floor under the wish). That room, about one birth in eighteen, is what a drum's leave for thirds is issued against: the heirless name an heir for their place, usually a kinsman's child not yet born, and the rest goes by the draw, by sale or by a block's say.
- **Extra children make the placeless, not more households.** A drum can have only as many households with children as it has places. When more families have thirds than heirless places make room for, the extras stay placeless (The placeless) and the drum levels off instead of compounding. A steady drum's ~3% of births outside the allowance put it near the top of this table: ~2.07 children a couple, ~2.7% over par, about one adult in forty placeless (`models/placing.py`). The lower rows are a drum that has let its line go. The family-tree model, from a drum at par:

| Children per couple | Settles over par | Adults over 45 placeless | Levels off in |
|---|---|---|---|
| ~2.01 (canon: 12% thirds) | 0 | almost none | — |
| 2.25 | ~19% | ~1 in 11 | ~200 yr |
| 2.5 | ~38% | ~1 in 6 | ~300 yr |
| 3.0 | ~70% | ~1 in 3 | ~250 yr |

- **The line rule is what holds it.** Where a place goes instead to whichever relative has waited longest, crowding only delays everyone's household and the drum keeps growing, or swings: at three children a couple the model's seniority drum ran ~50% over par and then ~25% under, on a cycle of about 180 years. Where everyone gets a place of their own, as in drums with per-person places, extra children found households and growth compounds: at 2.25 children a couple the model's drum tripled in 400 years.
- **Shares drift to careful families; places don't.** A childless person's share goes to the next in line in that branch of the family, so prolific lines grow thin on wallets. Places can't concentrate: each household holds one, and a childless household's place goes to a niece, a nephew or an adopted heir. Adopting an adult heir, as Japanese families adopted sons-in-law, is the normal way to keep a holding, and the only way to give a share outside the family.
- **An elder can hand over a place early.** Central European farmers retired on a maintenance contract, the *Altenteil*: the heir took the farm and the old couple kept a room and their keep. In a drum it moves the wait from the young to the old.
- **The wait for a place is bridged in many ways.** A couple without a place lives in a parent's or grandparent's place, rents rooms from an old couple whose children have households of their own, lodges where it works, takes a place an elder hands down early, swaps into another drum, or, in an open drum, borrows a Margin flat for up to five years (open-drums.md). A drum of 200 M holds all of these at once, and families choose by temperament and by who they have. Holdings drums without a Margin have only the family routes, rent and a small pool of heirless places.
- **The place count sets the drum's size.** A household has its children once in its tenure, so a drum settles at about 2¼ people a place. One with fewer places shrinks to fit them; one with more leaves the spares empty, in the drum's pool.
- **The drum keeps a small pool** for places and shares with no heir and for orphans. New metal doesn't join it: it founds new drums at the raft's edge (economy.md).

### The placeless
The placeless are adults who never head a household: the placeless-born, who were born outside a household's allowance and have no claim on a place (families.md § Born to a place); the few born to a place whom no place or marriage reached; and people who choose a house instead. In a steady drum they are about one adult in forty, nearly all of them placeless-born, and many more in a drum that has loosened its line. Historical non-heirs had hard lives for reasons the Shade has removed: partnership was respectable only in marriage, they had no income of their own, and they lived on the heir's sufferance in a back room.

- **Partnership without children.** The placeless can marry and live together, since birth control separates partnership from children. Only children belong to a place.
- **A living of their own.** The commons is per head and full-time work pays ~80% of a wallet (Places and shares), so the placeless aren't poor, and those born to a place keep their shares. The placeless-born mostly have none, and work. No historical non-heir had as much.
- **Houses.** A second tenure, held by bodies that don't die (orders, crews, colleges, hall guilds), entered by election, not birth, with rooms for members, halls in common and no children raised in them. Beguinages are the model: lay women who lived in common, kept their own property and could leave to marry. So are Oxford and Cambridge colleges, whose fellows mostly had to stay unmarried until 1882, and monasteries. A house gives a home, property in common, standing and companions for life. It has to be unmistakably not a place: membership for life, but no heir and no nursery. In open drums, Margin flats lent for life to placeless couples do the same (open-drums.md).
- **Work with children.** Fosterage and schools. Early medieval Ireland sent children to be raised in another household into their teens, and the bond was lifelong. The table of aunts gets real content: the aunt who fosters a niece from seven to fourteen, or the house that runs the block's school.
- **Offices of trust.** People without heirs make good keepers of a commons: the Church imposed celibacy partly so that offices and property couldn't pass to priests' children. The Ledger, the Register, the clinics and the beam booking, perhaps governance.md's order of keepers: a placeless life can be the one the drum trusts most.
- **A door left open.** A placeless adult can still marry an heir or be adopted into a heirless place, even late: with banked gametes, a place at fifty can still bring a child. For the placeless-born that is a placing, and it spends the room a third child would have had, so the door is narrow: about one in ten (families.md § Placing).
- **Not a caste, mostly.** Placelessness ends with the person where the placeless raise no children, and most don't. Earth's landless underclasses were hereditary because cottagers had children, and the Shade's version of that is the line outside (below).

What isn't clean:
- **Choosing the heir.** The sharpest edge in every stem society: birth order (primogeniture, or ultimogeniture in parts of England), the holders' choice, Japan's habit of adopting the ablest son-in-law, or lot. Each gives a drum a different politics, and each leaves someone passed over.
- **Waiting or settling.** An open door means some people spend decades hoping. A house that asks its members to renounce their claim to a place settles it cleanly, as entering religion ended a medieval monk's right to inherit. The choice between waiting and a house may be the defining decision of a placeless life.
- **Mortmain.** Bodies that never die accumulate land. England's Statute of Mortmain (1279) and the Dissolution of the Monasteries (1536–41) were fights over exactly this. Every place a house holds is one fewer household with children, so a drum's rule on what houses may hold is a rule on how many children it has.
- **The line outside.** What holds a household to its allowance is a chance of a third to lose. The placeless-born have no place and no draw, so nothing holds them, and two of them can have a child, who is placeless-born in turn. Most don't: keeping the norm asks for contraception, not abstinence, and people want children once they have a home to pass on. But the few who do are the next generation's parents. Mixed in with everyone else they stay a trace, ~0.2% of a drum's births; where they become a people apart, marrying their own, their line takes a tenth of a drum's births in ~500–1,500 years. Drums keep the placeless-born mixed in, place their children, charge a keep or fit them, and each answer costs something (families.md § The line outside). When 19th-century Bavaria restricted marriage among the poor, many births simply moved outside marriage.

### Other systems
| | Holdings (common) | Pool with birth rights | Custom and orders | Coercion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A child's share comes from | A death in the family | The drum's pool, spending a parent's right (economy.md) | The pool, by norm | The drum, by order |
| Big families are | By leave: lucky, well connected or rich; a third born without it is placeless-born | Rich, connected or lucky | Disapproved of | Punished |
| Who stays childless | ~5%: householders with no heir, and the placeless | Sellers of rights, where sales are allowed | Orders and the honoured | Whoever the drum says |
| How it fails | The assembly votes the line away, or a line outside grows; then a Dark finds the overspent | Shareless children; the poor selling their own child's right | Norms weaken and the drum overshoots | Revolt, or other drums turning on it |

- **Pool drums** (Sorrel, Lovage) are reformers' drums: shares return to a pool at death and places are allotted by age or auction. They're equal between families, and they need a registry and an answer for the shareless child.
- **Unspent rights go back to the pool (proposal).** A right whose holder dies before spending it returns to the pool and is reissued, by lottery to couples who have spent theirs. Without that rule a pool drum loses the rights of everyone who dies without spending them, the ~0.7% a generation who die before having children and the childless who never give theirs away, and it shrinks; after a plague it would never refill. With it, the pool keeps births equal to deaths, and the lottery is where a pool drum's third children come from, beside the rights the childless give to kin and friends. Couples who want a third still gain by both, by exactly the room the childless and the early dead leave (families.md § The draw). A child born without a right is the pool drums' placeless-born: no share from the pool, and no right of its own to spend.
- **Open drums** (open-drums.md) are holdings drums where custom does work: a Margin of unused shares and drum-held places, flats lent to couples until one of them inherits and to placeless couples for life, the gift of a share and a place by adoption, leave for a third given by the block, and tables of aunts around every child, most of them adults whose own children are grown, and the placeless.
- **The kinds of drum,** by how a household passes (whole to one heir, with portions, split among all, kept by the siblings), how leave for a third is given (the draw, gift, sale, a block's say) and where the line is drawn (rented rooms, a later place, the keep, the door), are set out in families.md.
- **Custom has one outlet fewer** than most Earth societies leaned on: there's nowhere to emigrate. Swaps between rafts free no shares, and new shares come one at a time. Stem places do the rest. The placeless don't have children, and houses, callings with real standing, honours for the childless (Hollow hours, a high-deck flat) and offices of trust give them a life worth having (The placeless).
- **Coercive drums** (counted drums) appear where a drum has overshot and nobody will bail it out. See Counted drums below.

### Counted drums
A counted drum decides who may have a child, and enforces it. People call them that for the Count, the board at every station ring that shows people against shares.

**Why drums count.**
- **Deaths set the births.** A nation drum loses ~1.7 M people a year, so that's how many children it can have.

| Children per couple | Long-run growth | 10% over par in | Lean 1 kWe life in | With stem places, levels off at |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.25 | 0.34%/yr | ~64 yr | ~250 yr | ~19% over (~1.7 kWe) |
| 2.5 | 0.64%/yr | ~41 yr | ~150 yr | ~38% over (~1.45 kWe) |
| 3.0 | 1.16%/yr | ~25 yr | ~95 yr | ~70% over (~1.2 kWe) |

- **Stem places cap overshoot, but not always low enough.** The first four columns are for a drum whose extra children found households of their own. Where children belong to places, the extras stay placeless and the drum levels off within a few centuries (Places and shares), but at 2.5 children a couple it levels off near where danger starts, and at 3.0 past it. So counting still comes when a drum lets its line go.
- **Overshoot is slow to show.** The growth rates are long-run. A drum at par is three or four generations deep, and when couples start having more children only the newest cohort is bigger, so the first decades grow at about a third of the long-run rate. The Count barely moves while the bigger cohorts are being born, and by the time it does, they're already here.

- **Overshoot is survivable for a long time.** At 10% over, each person has ~1.8 kWe and the 55-day reserve covers ~50 days; real danger starts near 1.5–2× par. So counting starts over the trend, not hunger, and every counted drum overstates its emergency.
- **The raft bails out disasters, not overbreeding.** A 0.1 AU raft gains ~8 × 10¹⁰ shares a year, so a drum that loses 10 M shares' worth of habitat to an accident can be rehoused from about an hour of the raft's growth, spread across ~4 × 10⁷ drums by tube. Nobody hands shares to a drum that bred past its own, or every drum would.
- **Holdings drums hide overshoot** in thin households and crowded family places until a blight or a Dark hits them all at once, like Ireland in 1845. Most counted drums start as overspent holdings drums after a bad year.
- **Raft-wide shocks** (a Dark, a raft-wide blight) leave nobody with slack, so many drums tip into counting together.
- **Standing can't shrink a drum.** The allowance holds a drum where it is. A drum that has to come down must cut births below deaths, which means fewer than two children a household, and people who will wait decades for a third won't wait for a first: they have it, and a drum that calls most of a generation placeless-born has no line left (families.md § Loosening and tightening). So a drum that must shrink licenses births and fits its people.
- **Some count one group.** On Earth most coercive sterilisation targeted minorities and the poor. The Shade's obvious targets are refuser families and their wild-born, and the placeless-born: a steady drum that fits its placeless-born until they are placed has ended its line outside and begun counting one group. A drum at par can still count them.

**The ladder.** Drums usually climb one rung at a time. Rungs 1 and 2 are not counting: the keep is what most steady drums charge for a child born outside the allowance, and penalties are a strict drum's answer to the same child; the Count proper begins at rung 3. Above rung 4, neighbours stop calling it a drum's own business.

| Rung | What the drum does | Earth echo | How others see it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 The keep | A posted price for the keep of a child born outside the allowance, from the Mill-table credit to its whole commons (economy.md § Children); proofreading free for children born to a place, charged for others | England's bastardy bonds; Singapore's "Stop at Two" fees | Normal: most steady drums post one |
| 2 Penalty | Parents of a child born outside the allowance lose wallet, Hollow hours, deck seniority, posts | China's "social maintenance fees" | Harsh but legitimate |
| 3 The fitting | A reversible implant for everyone at fourteen, removed at the clinic on licence | China's mandatory IUDs | Contested; many call it reasonable in a crisis |
| 4 Forced procedures | Unlicensed pregnancies ended; sterilisation after a quota | China 1983 (~21 M sterilisations), India 1976–77 (~8 M), Peru 1996–2000 (~270,000) | Criminal to most drums |
| 5 Unpersoning | Wild-born get no share, register entry, school or commons, or are taken and fostered | China's *heihaizi*; Romania's orphanages | Atrocity; neighbours act |
| 6 Removing the living | Expulsion onto neighbours' lanes; a fixed age of death; death lotteries | Tikopia's sea voyages; Tokugawa *mabiki*; Trollope's *The Fixed Period* | A small Burn: every drum's enemy |

- **Rung 3 is the typical counted drum:** licences by lottery, the fitting, and a birth stop of one to two decades. It's what a reform council, not a tyrant, ends up with.
- **Long lives tempt rung 6.** A full stop clears 10% overshoot in ~12 years at 120-year lives, ~40 at 400. A fixed age of 100 in a 120-year drum would remove a sixth of it at once.
- **Expulsion barely works.** A share doesn't travel, so neighbours gain mouths and no metal, and they can refuse to catch the cars. Dumping people on the Glide is an attack on the next drum.
- **Control is easy in a drum.** The clinics see nearly every pregnancy, the commons meters show a household eating for five on four shares within weeks, the register records every share and heir, and nine station rings are the only doors. Against it: 200 M people can hide a lot, home births need no machines, and every drum hears how others live.
- **The quiet nightmare** is a counted drum that edits embryos toward wanting fewer children, and stops needing implants.

**Life in one.** Rue, across the rows from Sorrel on Herb Row (ashgrove.md), is nine years into a stop.
- It was a holdings drum that voted its line away. A generation before the blight its assembly ruled that rented rooms counted as places, so a third born without leave could rent, marry and raise children of its own, and since nearly every couple wants a third, three children were ordinary within the decade. At ~14% over, a *Fusarium* blight took two vat lines, its thin households went short for a season, and the council that came out of that year posted the first Count and cut births to a quarter of deaths. That is less than one child a couple, and no drum has held that by standing, so Rue fits.
- Its Count now reads ~217 M people on 200 M shares (and 90 M places), ~8.5% over, 450,000 licences against ~1.66 M deaths a year: par in ~14 years. People bet on the date. Deaths are fewer than 217 M ÷ 120 would suggest, because the extra people are young, and the bettors who know it win.
- At fourteen every child goes to the clinic for the fitting, now a rite with a family dinner. Licences go by lottery to couples with a place, first children first, and can't be sold.
- Low-deck schools stand half-empty; in fifteen years Rue will have a hollow in its workforce.
- The well-off have Lovage births: they buy a birth right in Lovage and place the child there with relatives. Poorer couples hide pregnancies, and Rue's wild-born are mostly theirs.
- Rue's extra people are mostly shareless children, so its shares still carry full wallets, and the childfree swap in while would-be parents swap out. Each swap makes its rule a little more chosen.

**Legitimacy.** Counting holds when the Count is public and falling, the burden falls equally by lottery, it follows a shock people remember, it has an end date, and people can leave. It fails when the powerful are exempt, it outlives the shock as a bureaucracy's income, it targets one group, or the rings are shut. China's one-child rule lasted 35 years, past its own rationale; India's sterilisation drive cost the government the 1977 election.

**The right of the Glide.** Most rafts guarantee that anyone may glide out of any drum. That keeps most counted drums at rungs 2–3, since the people most likely to resist leave. But a share doesn't travel, so leaving without a swap makes someone a shareless lodger elsewhere: exit works for those who can find a swap, and fails the poor, who are counted hardest. A raft without the right, where drums can shut their rings, is where the dark drums are.

**What neighbours and the raft do.**

| Tool | Held by | Used against |
|---|---|---|
| Refuge swaps: shares from the pool for people fleeing | Neighbour drums | Rungs 4–6 |
| Placing wild-born children, or buying them rights | Neighbours, charities | Rung 5 |
| Gate embargo on produce, strains and parts | Neighbours | Rungs 3+ |
| Clinicians refusing licence checks, by Shade-wide norm | The medical guild | Rungs 3–4 |

- **The raft sanctions; it doesn't dock beam** for anything less than rung 6, since that would undo the fixed allotments this section rests on.
- **Neighbours are tempted to pay for coercion.** A counted drum at rung 3 keeps its people home; one that collapses sends them onto the Glide. Drums beside a counted one often quietly prefer it to count. Herb Row talks about Rue that way.

**How common.** Share counting ≈ how often a drum hits a serious overshoot or shock × the chance it answers by coercion × how long that lasts.

| | Rare | Canon | Common |
|---|---|---|---|
| Serious overshoot or shock, per drum | 1 / 3,000 yr | 1 / 1,000 yr | 1 / 300 yr |
| Answered by coercion | 10% | 30% | 50% |
| How long it lasts | 20 yr | 40 yr | 80 yr |
| Share of drums counting | ~0.07% | **~1%** | ~13% |

- **About one in every glide metro,** so nearly everyone has a counted drum a glide or two away, and about one drum in 800 is at rung 5–6. Earth's 1950–2020 rate was ~0.6% of state-years.
- **Waves.** After a Dark or a raft-wide blight the share on that raft can reach 10–30% for a generation. A story set just after a Dark has counted drums on every row.
- **Why drums still tip.** With the wish saturated nobody waits on a slow climb. A drum tips when its assembly loosens the line (rooms counted as places, a wider door, a third for everyone "this once"), when a line outside has grown for a thousand years, or when a shock finds it thin, and the canon rate folds these together (families.md § Loosening and tightening). Long lives slow every repair.
- **Every old drum has counted at some point:** at the canon rate, ~40 years in every 3,000. Most keep an old Count board in a museum. Drums are founded continuously at the rafts' edges as metal arrives (~400 a year on a 0.1 AU raft; economy.md), so a raft always holds drums of every age, from a few centuries to as old as the Shade, and the young ones have no Count to remember. They are few: one to four drums in 100,000 are under 10,000 years old, and their founders came from old drums.

### The clinics and the wild-born
- **Proofreading makes nearly every birth visible.** In a pool drum the clinic is where the right is checked; in a holdings drum it records which household the child belongs to, whether it was born to a place, and whose share, if any, it carries.
- **The wild-born** are children conceived and carried outside the clinics: unproofread, unregistered, often shareless. Since proofreading is a norm and not a law, some are the children of refusers, not lawbreakers. People confuse the two. A placeless-born child is a different thing: born in the clinic, registered and proofread, and outside only its household's allowance.
- **A drum that wants control** can make proofreading free for children born to a place and charge for the rest, without punishing anyone outright.

### Leaving
Emigration is no outlet: a few go, and it's hard. Someone short of floor and food can't afford the trip, and a drum won't let metal leave.

| Trip | Cost per person | Time | What else it takes |
|---|---|---|---|
| To a passing raft | A 100–300 km/s capsule throw, ~10–90 GJ (raft-transport.md): ~2,800–25,000 kWh, 4 months to 3 years of a wallet | Days, but only to rafts passing within a few AU | A catch ramp on the other raft, and a share there, so in practice a swap |
| To a chosen raft across the Shade | The same throw | Thousands of years at ~300 km/s across ~2 pc | A habitat for the trip: not a capsule ride |
| To the frontier, in person | Climbing back out to 100–200 pc against the falling stream: ~80 GJ/kg, ~1.6 × 10⁸ kWh for ~7 t of person, shield and life support (~20,000 years of a wallet) | ~1 Myr, the payloads' fall run backwards (matter-delivery.md) | A self-supporting habitat, so a whole drum leaves together, taking its metal |
| To the frontier, as a genome | Nearly free: a genome is information | Centuries by laser | An outpost willing to grow and raise the child |

- **Most emigrants are swaps with passing rafts,** arranged across light-minutes to light-days for the few days a raft is near.
- **A drum leaving whole** for the frontier is a once-in-an-era event, and its metal walks out with it, so the raft has a say.
- **The cheap way to the frontier is to send a child, not go.** A would-be emigrant can sign over a genome; an outpost grows and raises the child. Whether outposts want Shade children, and whether this counts as a birth anywhere, is open.

### What births do to the gene pool
- **Selection needs uneven family sizes, and a drum can't avoid some.** About one couple in twenty has no children, so someone's extra children must replace them, and the families who have those children gain on the rest by at least the room the childless leave (families.md § The floor under the wish). A strict two-each drum barely evolves only if it replaces its childless with children nobody chose. Holdings and tradable rights let some families have more, and over the Shade's ~3 × 10⁶ generations even weak selection finishes its work. It finished this piece long ago: most couples in every drum want a third.
- **Selection counts grandchildren, not children.** An extra child adds descendants only if it heads a household of its own: Lack's principle, first worked out for birds' clutch sizes. Where every child stands alike, a third heads a household as often as a first or a second, since heirs marry by age and not by their partner's birth order, and a three-child family leaves three to a two-child family's two. Children in a drum are cheap and nearly all grow up, so no cost in kWh ever outweighed that, and the wish for more climbed until it reached nearly everyone (families.md § The floor under the wish). What stops an extra child paying is a rule that it heads no household: born outside the allowance, it is placeless-born, and the line rule passes over it. Then a household leaves more grandchildren by waiting for leave than by having a child outside, so long as fewer of the placeless-born are placed than households are given thirds (about one in eight) and a household with a child outside is out of the draw. So over the Shade's age selection has tuned two things. One is when people want children: once they have a home of their own to pass on, and waiting costs a 120-year life nothing. The other is which children they want: ones they can place. How many, they go on wanting more.
- **The unselected already do something like it.** In US data, rising house prices raise births among homeowners and lower them among renters (Dettling and Kearney 2014). The cue is a home of one's own, not just a roof, which is why houses and lent flats must be unmistakably not places.
- **How the Shade got here,** from three models (`models/regimes.py`, `models/ratchet.py`, `models/share_market.py`, written up in `models/ratchet.md`), each starting from a fresh spread of wishes. Under stem places with no line drawn at birth, every child's chance of heading a household is the same, the ratio of adult slots to children: ~0.9 when the drum is 16% over par, ~0.8 at 29%, whether heirs marry by age or by share; heirs who choose by portion take it only to ~0.85 and ~0.65, since most households come down the line rule at a death, not through a marriage. A rule that ranks children by family size, or rations thirds by lot among those who want one, brought the advantage down to the floor, but no lower:

| How births were held | First Count from par | Where the wish went |
|---|---|---|
| Rented rooms raise children | ~300–450 yr | Compounds |
| Custom (stem places) | ~450–1,000 yr, then two-fifths to a half of later years counting | Climbs ~1 sd every 800–2,000 yr |
| Portions or birth order deciding every household | ~600–1,400 yr | Climbs, more slowly as thirds spread |
| A posted price, or a market in rights | Never | Numbers held; the wish's strength climbs ~1 sd every 800–2,000 yr, and the price with it |
| A draw for thirds among couples who want one, or a pool's lottery | Never | Saturates: half of couples want a third after ~1,500–3,800 yr, four in five or more after ~10,000, and about one in eight gets one. This is where every drum now is |
| The drum's own children: the childless replaced by embryos drawn from every family | Never | Doesn't climb |

  The parent–child regression of the wish is taken at 0.5–0.2, bracketing twin studies' ~0.2–0.4 for completed fertility, plus culture; a fast churn of fashions would lower it, and everything would run slower. Every row but the last ends with most couples wanting a third, and the Shade is older than the slowest of them.
- **So drums choose who is given a third, and where the line is drawn.** Leave by lot makes a third a lucky family's, by gift a well-connected one's, by sale a rich one's, and by a block's say the neighbours' favourite's; portions put a third's cost on the younger children of big families, and sibling households on whoever leaves. Most old drums draw, since the lot gives every household the same chance to lose and breeds nothing but entering. Medicine sets the room: where one couple in fifty is childless instead of one in twenty, one family in twenty is given a third (families.md). Drums differ too on children born in rented rooms (Jeff, 6 Nov): some ban it with varying severity, some frown on it and the children grow up poorer, some don't mind, and those run well above par, with the rents and the thin commons that follow. In families.md's terms the first call such a child placeless-born for good, the second give it a later place, and the third have no line.
- **Where children can outrun places, the numbers go at once.** Per-person places, rooms that count as places or a door left wide let children born outside the allowance found households, and since everyone already wants more children, such a drum has three a couple within a generation. Those drums overshoot, and count.
- **Callings and houses select against whatever draws people to them, but only weakly:** most of the placeless are placeless by birth, not temperament.
- **The line outside is where selection still runs.** Among the placeless-born a child costs nothing they have, and the few who have children hand the habit down. It is the one place in a steady drum where wanting children badly, whatever one's standing, still gains (The placeless; families.md § The line outside).

## Open questions
- How often people move far across a raft, which sets whether a raft has regional looks among refusers.
- Whether frontier outposts take Shade children as genomes, and on what terms.
- How the Shade's age at story time shapes things: 10,000 years, 1 Myr and 100 Myr look different among refusers. (deliberately open)
- How drums choose an heir: birth order, the holders' choice, the ablest, or lot. Each gives a different politics. (deliberately open)
- What houses may hold, and how drums settle mortmain: every place a house holds is one fewer household with children.
- **How tightly the wish tunes:** answered in families.md (`models/regimes.py`, `models/ratchet.py`, `models/share_market.py`, `models/placing.py`): it doesn't; the wish is saturated in every drum, and drums hold their numbers by who is born to a place. Still open there: how many children are born outside the allowance and how many are placed, what keeps one couple in twenty childless, whether steady drums fit their placeless-born, and the keep in kWh.

## Method
- Generation ~35 years: 100 Myr ≈ 3 × 10⁶ generations.
- New mutations ~60–80 per child, ~2 more per year of the father's age; ~1–2 harmful. A drum makes ~2 × 10⁸ children a generation, ~1.4 × 10¹⁰ mutations, against ~9 × 10⁹ possible single-letter changes.
- Neutral fixation ~4N generations. Divergence between drums by chance F_ST ≈ 1/(1 + 4Nm), ~10⁻⁸ for N = 2 × 10⁸, m = 0.1.
- An advantage s spreads across a grid at ~σ√(2s) per generation: ~0.14 drums a generation at σ = 1 drum, s = 0.01, ~4 × 10⁶ generations across 600,000 drums. Well mixed: ~(2/s) ln 2N ≈ 9,000 generations.
- An unselected gene is knocked out by mutation pressure in ~10⁵–10⁶ generations.
- Unchecked growth: three children a couple, ~1.5× a generation, ~1.2% a year in the long run. From a drum at par, a cohort model (Gompertz mortality fitted to 120-year lives, births at 35 ± 6) gives ~0.4% a year for the first 20 years, 10% over in ~25 years and doubling in ~95.
- Places (stem, Nov 2026): a family-tree model (`models/stem_places.py`) of ~15,000 people, run 300 years to build a genealogy, then 600 with places on and 100–400 more for each change. Lives are Gompertz–Makeham with a mean of ~119 years and survival to 35 of 0.993. People marry at 28.5 ± 3 whatever their places, at random within ~5 years of age and outside first cousins. A couple has its children 3 and 6 years after it has a household, a third in 12% of families and none in 5%, and none once the younger partner is past 60. A place passes at its last holder's death to the eldest descendant aged 18–45 without a household, then the same in the holder's siblings' lines, then a child of either line, then the drum's eldest adult without a household aged 18–55 (an adopted heir). Over three runs the drum settles at ~2.2 people a place, so 200 M people need ~90 M places of 35 × 2.22 ≈ 78 m² of home. Adult heirs inherit at ~27 (10–90%: 21–32) and ~14% of places go to children. 61% stay in the holder's line (95% of those to great-grandchildren), 29% go to a sibling's line and 10% to adopted heirs. Spouses join at ~29 and first children come at ~32. Three weddings in four have a place; the rest wait a median of ~3 years, ~28% at five and ~2% at ten. Started at 2.4–2.6 people a place, the drum shrank to ~2.2; started at 1.9, it held its numbers and a fifth of its places stood empty.
- Rhythm: a place lands three generations down, so an heir's age plus three parents' ages at a birth (~33 each) comes to the holders' age at the last death, ~122: heirs at ~24–27. The old rule of thumb for per-person places, A ≈ (L − 3k) ÷ (k + 1), gave places at ~28.
- Per-person places, the earlier canon (1 Nov 2026): people per place L ÷ (L − A) = 1.33 at A = 30, so 150 M places of ~47 m². The earlier family-tree model (marriage at ~28, children 3 and 6 years later, places to the eldest placeless descendant of the dead) gave first places at 30.4 (10–90%: 21–40) and 1.34 people a place; 71% of places came from a great-grandparent. The stem model's code run with per-person places reproduces ~1.3 people a place and first places at ~26–31.
- Extra children, stem places: from a drum at par, couples are switched to more children (thirds, and fourths for 3.0). At 2.25, 2.5 and 3.0 children a couple the drum levels off at ~19%, ~38% and ~70% over par after ~200, ~300 and ~250 years, with ~9%, ~17% and ~31% of adults over 45 placeless. That's about one and a half times the excess births (12%, 24%, 49%), since adoption and late places still settle some of the placeless. Places to the longest-waiting relative instead (seniority): 30% thirds reached +19% after 300 years and was still rising, and 3.0 children a couple swung between ~+50% and ~−25% on a ~180-year cycle. Per-person places: 2.25 children a couple tripled the drum in 400 years.
- The floor: at par the households that have children must average 2 ÷ 0.95 ≈ 2.1 children who head households; a family of two leaves at most 2, so a family with a third leaves at least 0.105 ÷ p more, p the share of fertile families with a third (families.md § Method).
- The ratchet (`models/ratchet.py`, output in `models/ratchet-results.txt`): a cohort model of the drum with a latent wish for a third, normal at founding; households above a threshold have a third; each cohort's mean rises by b × the selection differential, b = 0.2–0.5. Custom: every child's chance of a household f = 1 ÷ (1 + excess births), so w3 = 3f and w2 = 2f (S = 0.087 sd at par); ranked: w3 = 2 + 0.105 ÷ p; the draw: the same for couples who want a third, with births held at par; a price or market: the most eager tenth pay. Overshoot is the stock of placeless, 1.5× the excess births (above); a Count at 10% over clears to 2% under over ~15 years by lottery, never licensing fewer than half the par births, and pauses selection. First Count from par: custom 450–990 years (600–1,430 with rent pushing back), ranked 620–1,430, rented rooms 290–440; after it, 18–50% of the remaining years counting.
- The share market (`models/share_market.py`, outputs in `models/out/`): `stem_places.py`'s family tree with the wish raised to 2.18 or 2.38 children a couple, settled 300 years over par, every household whose brood is born in a 30-year window tagged and followed 120 years; four marriage markets (by age; heirs take only share-holders; like marries like by share; heirs take the largest portion). Each child's chance of a household is 0.93–0.95 at 2.18 and 0.83–0.85 at 2.38 in two-child homes, the same for a three-child home's first two, and 3–8 points less for its third; a third's advantage is 0.89–0.94 and 0.76–0.82 by age or share, 0.84–0.85 and 0.65 by portion.
- The regimes (`models/regimes.py`): a generation model of ~4,000 places with each inheritance and birth rule in families.md. Rules that rank children by family size, or draw thirds by lot among couples who want one, reach the floor by construction, since they place every child of a two-child family first; the model measures the other rules against it (families.md § Method).
- Born to a place (`models/placing.py`, output in `models/out/placing.txt`): room = 1 − 0.95 × 0.993 ≈ 5.7% of a drum's births, a third for ~12% of fertile families. With 3% of births outside the allowance and one in ten of those placed, thirds by leave fall to one family in 8.8, the drum sits 2.7% over par and 2.6% of its people are placeless-born: in a nation drum ~50,000 born outside a year, ~5,000 placed, ~90,000 thirds by leave, and ~205 M people on 200 M shares. A household with a child outside gains (the share placed) − (its chance of leave, ~0.12): −0.12 with the door shut and about nothing at one in ten placed. The line outside: one placeless-born couple in 25 having three children gives ~0.2% of a drum's births while they pair at random; a line that keeps apart reaches a tenth of births in ~500–1,500 years if the habit passes down strongly (b = 0.8–0.5), and in ~1,100 years at b = 0.35 if old lines marry only old lines (families.md § Method).
- Shares: in the stem model at par (shares reset to one a person after the burn-in), ~60% of babies are born before the death that frees their share and wait ~6 years (90th percentile ~12). About 3% of people are shareless, almost all children. ~40% of shares come from a great-great-grandparent, ~30% from a great-grandparent and ~25% from an aunt's or cousin's line. The same code with per-person places gives ~47–51% born shareless, a ~4.4-year wait and ~1.8% shareless, against the earlier model's ~60%, 3.6–3.9 years and ~1.5%. So share timing depends on how a freed share is matched to a family's next child, and stem places lengthen the wait by about a year and a half.
- Families: replacement is 2 ÷ (survival to 35) ≈ 2 ÷ 0.993 ≈ 2.01 births a couple. With a share c of adults childless, parents average 2.01 ÷ (1 − c): at c = 5%, 2.12, so ~12% of families have a third child if none stop at one. A third in 15% of families needs c ≈ 6%.
- Pool drums: a right is lost if its holder dies before spending it; survival to 35 is 0.993, so ~0.7% of rights a generation.
- Inheritance: each partner has 4 great-grandparent couples, and each couple 8 great-grandchildren, one of whom inherits its place, so on average a couple's 16 great-grandparents free 4 × ⅛ × 2 = 1 place for it. Each great-grandparent has 16 great-great-grandchildren, so each death frees, on average, a share for a great-great-grandchild.
- Overspending: four wallets of 21.6 kWh across five people ≈ 17.3 kWh a head. Work: 3.1 kWh an hour × 2,000 hours ÷ 365 ≈ 17 kWh a day.
- Leaving: wallet ~0.9 kW ≈ 7,900 kWh a year. Climbing out to the frontier ≈ ½(v_arrival² − v_orbit²) ≈ ½(440² − 160²) km²/s² ≈ 84 GJ/kg, the catch figure run backwards; × 7 t ≈ 5.9 × 10¹⁴ J ≈ 1.6 × 10⁸ kWh. Crossing ~2 pc at 300 km/s ≈ 6,500 years.
- Germline doubling dose ~1 Gy; ~1 mSv/yr × 30 years ≈ 30 mSv, ~3% of the spontaneous rate (~2 of ~70 new mutations). Cancer: ICRP 103's 5.5% per Sv × ~0.12 Sv ≈ 0.7%. The ~20 mSv/yr once used here is the dose just inside the jacket, before the moat and decks (habitat-design.md).
- Counted drums: growth (children ÷ 2)^(1/35 yr) − 1; 10% over in ln 1.1 ÷ rate; 1 kWe in ln 2 ÷ rate. Rue: three children a couple takes a drum at par to 228 M (14% over) in ~35 years (~50 at 2.5); births at a quarter of deaths for 9 years ≈ −1.2 M a year → ~217 M. The extra people are young, so deaths stay near ~1.66 M a year, not 217 M ÷ 120 ≈ 1.8 M; at 450,000 licences the drum falls ~1.2 M a year and reaches par in ~14 years. All from the cohort model above. Raft growth 1.4 × 10⁻⁹ a year × 6 × 10¹⁹ ≈ 8.4 × 10¹⁰ shares a year; 10 M shares ≈ 1 hour of it, or a year's growth of ~3.6 × 10⁷ drums (a patch ~150,000 km across). Prevalence = rate × probability × duration; Earth ≈ 75 coercive state-years ÷ (~190 states × 70 yr).
