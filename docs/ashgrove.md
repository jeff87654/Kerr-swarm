# Ashgrove: a neighbourhood and a family in the Shade

*Narrative sketch from a working session, Oct 2026. Four days with one family in a nation drum on a raft in the Shade. Built on raft-habitat.md (the drum, its districts and the Hollow), drum-transport.md (cabs and Spindle lifts), daily-life.md (the 2 kWe budget and prices), economy.md (dividends, wages, places, birth rights, the Dark as a sentence), raft-transport.md (the Glide and cyclers), food-system.md and cuisine.md (the Mill and Vat tables, blights, the gin problem), full-swarm.md (the Lantern and the beam) and matter-delivery.md (where the metal comes from). Numbers come from those docs. Everything with a name is invented, and where the docs leave a choice open the sketch picks one; both are listed under What the sketch assumes. Speech and names are given in translation. Revised Oct 2026 for the 3 km nation drum (raft-habitat.md): Sorrel now holds 200 million, Ashgrove sits on decks 73–78 at the same weight, and the glide to Lovage takes 140 seconds.*

## At a glance
- **Where:** Ashgrove, about 3,000 people on decks 73–78 of Sorrel, a nation drum of 200 million on Herb Row of Merrow, a 0.1 AU raft of ~6 × 10¹⁹ people in the middle of the Shade.
- **Who:** the Daces. Anselm (52) mends things. Teo (44) works the Gate at Station Ring 3, where anything alive comes into the drum. Wren (15) plays a heliox flute and has gone dark for twelve days to pay for a trip. Ossian (8) has never seen a star. Anselm's mother Hedda (93) spent forty years on the radiator crews behind the drum, and is one of the few people in Sorrel who has.
- **The days:** a working day; the family's turn in the Hollow; a wedding one glide away in Lovage; Ossian's visit to the Window.
- **Neighbours, different rules.** Sorrel allots its high decks and Hollow hours by age, and birth rights can be given but not sold. Lovage, next door, auctions both and lets rights be sold. They sit at different points on the gin curve too.
- **New here:** galleries behind the drum where every Sorrel child sees the stars once, and the Eleven Days, a beam gap within living memory after which Merrow ran its cyclers fast.

## Where they live

### Merrow and Herb Row
Merrow is a compact raft about 0.1 AU across: some 6 × 10¹⁹ people in about 300 billion nation drums, standing 24.5 km apart on a square grid with their axes toward the Lantern. It lies in the middle of the Shade, on an orbit that takes ~39,000 years, and its beam comes from a patch of the Lantern about eleven light-months away. Merrow, not any one drum, holds the beam contract, and nearly a year of its power is always in flight toward it.

A drum's formal address is a pair of grid numbers that nobody says aloud. A long stretch of one row was founded together and named for herbs. Sorrel's neighbours along Herb Row are Tansy and Lovage, a 140-second glide either way; across the rows lie Rue and Chervil.

Each drum is a nation of about 200 million and sets its own rules inside the same beam per place (economy.md). Neighbours a free glide apart can differ a lot:
- **Sorrel** runs the standard commons and a full dividend. It allots its fixed goods by age, so the old get the high decks and the most Hollow time. Birth rights can be given but not sold. Gin is sold almost at cost, with thiamine in it, as in the salt.
- **Lovage** auctions its high decks and Hollow hours and spends the rents on a bigger commons, most visibly a sunline held at full brightness for twelve hours instead of six. Its birth rights can be sold. Spirits are taxed hard.
- **Rue,** across the rows, is a counted drum nine years into a birth stop (people.md). Its children are fitted with implants at fourteen, its Count is posted on every platform, and Herb Row is quietly glad it counts rather than collapses.
- **Chervil,** across the rows too, is an open drum (open-drums.md): 3% under par since a stop ~340 years ago, with cooked halls, guest-right and a Ledger at every station ring. Herb Row finds it lovely and a little pleased with itself.
- Sorrelers find Lovage dazzling and a little vulgar. Lovage finds Sorrel dim and a little drunk. Anyone who minds can swap homes; the Glide is free.

### Sorrel
Sorrel is raft-habitat.md's nation drum: 3 km in radius, 3.75 km long, turning once every 110 seconds, with ~375 decks from the 1-g rim to the half-g floor of the Hollow. Out is down. The Keel at the rim holds the food plant, the water, the reserves and the nine station rings where glide cars come and go, and nobody lives there. Families live on the Low decks, most people in the middle, and the old up near the Hollow, toward lighter steps, a lower dose and the open sky. In Lovage, people who have done well go up. In Sorrel the people who go up are the old, and "she's gone up" is a polite way of saying someone has retired.

### Ashgrove
Ashgrove is a neighbourhood on decks 73 to 78, about 2.7 km from the axis, where everything weighs nine-tenths of what it would on Earth. About 3,000 people live in a block roughly 230 m on a side, served by five lift cores.

Its middle is **the Court**, an atrium cut up through all six decks. Its floor, on deck 73, is about 90 by 60 m, and its ceiling is the underside of deck 79, 24 m up. Each deck above opens onto it as a ring of planted terraces. It's the only place in Ashgrove where the ceiling is higher than a room.

Everywhere else the decks are 4 m apart and the ceilings about 3 m. Ashgrove is an indoor town of arcades, lamp-lit about as brightly as a living room. **The Long Arcade** runs spinward across it on deck 75, with a slow trolley down the middle, and **Pear Street** runs along the axis and crosses it at the Court. Along them:
- **The Mill Hall** serves the Mill table free, three meals a day, from the commons: jook in the morning, cold glass noodles and starch jellies at noon, a stew with a swallow at night, a bowl of the Nine on every table and Mill lager on tap. Nobody in Sorrel goes hungry, and nobody under thirty admits to eating at the Hall by choice, though most of them do.
- **The baths** are the warmest room in Ashgrove. The water comes from the food plant's 65 °C waste-heat loop, so heating it is free; only pumping and cleaning cost anything. In heliox, which draws heat from skin about four times faster than air, a hot bath is the cheapest luxury in the drum.
- **The greengrocer's** is the nearest thing Ashgrove has to a jeweller's. Tomatoes sit in a glass case at half a kWh each, the price of a pint and a bit. Herbs are cheap and salad is a treat, and there are always a few orchard apples at ~4.4 kWh for people buying a gift.
- **Dace's,** Anselm's mending shop, between a clinic and a stair.
- **The school,** on deck 75, takes the children of Ashgrove and the blocks around it. Sorrel raises its children on the Low decks, near full weight, because children's bones grow best there, or so every parent in Sorrel believes.

**The Court's light** is Ashgrove's oldest argument. The neighbourhood's share of park lighting is ~53 W a head, ~160 kW in all. That lights the Court's floor to overcast-day brightness (10,000 lux) for twelve hours a day and leaves the terraces about a quarter of that, or spreads half that brightness evenly over the Court and all its terraces, about 1.5 ha. Every few years Ashgrove votes. The Court has won six times running, mostly because of the pears, and the terraces stay at a grey quarter-light. The people whose flats open onto the terraces have opinions about this.

**The pears.** There haven't been ash trees in Ashgrove for as long as anyone knows. There are three espaliered pear trees on the Court's brightest wall, older than anyone living. They get their light free, since the Court is lit anyway, and they're the only things in Ashgrove that get a winter: each year the school's youngest class helps wrap them in chilled tents for six weeks so they'll bloom, unwraps them, and a few weeks later dabs their blossoms with brushes. They give a few hundred small, hard pears a year. By custom the first two go to Ashgrove's oldest resident and its newest baby, and the rest go round by lottery.

**The air** is heliox, and the rooms run warm, since helium chills skin. Sound travels nearly twice as fast as in air, so voices come out bright and thin-edged and the Arcade rings. Nobody in Sorrel hears it that way. They hear it the other way round: recordings from the old air sound to them as though spoken through a blanket.

**Weight is a map.** Running spinward makes you heavier and running back makes you lighter, so children race antispinward and lose fights spinward. A dropped cup lands about two centimetres antispinward of where it was aimed.

**The calendar** is chosen, since nothing outside makes a day or a season. Ashgrove keeps the two big festivals. Booking Day, when Merrow learns its beam for the year ahead, is the harvest. Knotnight is the ring era's winter turned into one evening: the Court's lamps go down, and the neighbourhood makes the year's larder together and eats whatever's fresh, as if a long dark were coming. None has, for more than a hundred million years.

**Status.** Sorrel's dividends are equal and its fixed goods go by age, so rank lives elsewhere: in standing (posts held, years served), in what people make, and in their things. At ~65 years per kilogram, most of what anyone owns is older than they are, and the best chair in a room is the oldest.

## The Daces

**Anselm Dace,** 52, runs the mending shop. He relines coats, lets out children's trousers, re-springs chairs, resoles shoes and fixes cleaning robots. Paying him uses no energy, since the kWh just move from one wallet to his, while new fabric is mass at roughly 25 kWh a kilogram, so in effect he's paid to save his customers' metal. He charges what Ashgrove pays for an hour of anyone's time, about 3 kWh, a little more than a Vat-table dinner.

**Teodora (Teo) Dace,** 44, grew up in Lovage, in one of the dim flats on a Low deck, and swapped into Sorrel at 22 to marry Anselm. She works at the Gate on Station Ring 3, in the Keel: Sorrel's quarantine for anything biological coming into the drum. In a drum whose protein comes out of monoculture vats, a stray phage is a famine, and the Gate is where it gets stopped. The drum pays her ~17 kWh a day from a levy on everyone's dividend. She's good at the job and better with the people.

**Wren Dace,** 15, plays a heliox flute in the Ashgrove youth ensemble. Her best friend, Juno, is someone she has never met. Juno lives a million kilometres down the row, about 41,000 drums away, three and a third seconds by light. They met in a slow world, a game built to tolerate lag, and they talk the way people across a raft talk, with a seven-second wait after everything either of them says. Wren is going dark for twelve days, giving up her personal compute, to pay for an express cycler to Juno's drum at the long break.

**Ossian (Oss) Dace,** 8, is at the Ashgrove school. He loves the Glide, his grandmother and the idea of stars, which he has never seen. Nobody does from inside a drum: the raft's own layers hide the sky. Like many children in Sorrel, he's named for the person whose place he took, a lift engineer from deck 286 called Ossian Hale, whom the family never met. Every year on the date they send a note to Hale's daughter.

**Hedda Dace,** 93, Anselm's mother, lives alone on deck 361, a little over a minute up by cab, in one of the flats Sorrel allots to the old near the Hollow, where a fall carries half the energy it would at the rim. She spent forty years on the back crews, keeping up the radiator panels behind the drum, so she has looked at the stars on ordinary working days. The week Ossian was born, she asked to be the one who takes him to the Window.

### The flat
The Daces live on deck 77, off Pear Street, two corners from the Court: about 140 m², four rooms and a kitchen, the standard 35 m² of home a head. It has no windows. Almost nothing in Sorrel does, apart from the flats on the Court's terraces and the terraces of the Hollow's end walls. The lamps follow the drum's clock: a ramp up at half past six, a long even day at about 200 lux, a fade toward eleven.

What they own:
- **The table,** which was Hedda's grandmother's, and four chairs that don't match. The newest thing in the flat is Ossian's bed, from his sixth birthday.
- **The Lovage starter,** Teo's family sourdough, which her grandmother kept. It's registered with Sorrel's strain library, so it is both an heirloom and a file: if the crock died, the library could grow it back from its genome within a week. Teo would still mourn it.
- **The herb shelf,** a metre of basil, chives, shiso and coriander under a grow lamp, a watt or two of light per person. It's the first thing anyone in Sorrel plants and the last thing that goes dark.
- **The larder,** a cupboard of hard bread, aged cheese, preserves, miso, dried noodles and a tin of the Nine. The drum keeps the air going for 55 days without power; families keep the food, as a matter of pride.

### Dividends, wages and rights
Everyone in Sorrel draws the same dividend, ~21.6 kWh a day, on top of the commons that feeds, lights, schools and doctors them all. People say "kays". A child's dividend goes to the household; at fourteen Sorrel puts half of it in the child's own name, and all of it at eighteen. Banked kWh are lent to whoever is spending now, and come back, without interest, within the booking year.

The Daces, in kWh a day, averaged over a month:

| | Usually | This fortnight |
|---|---|---|
| **In:** four dividends | 86.4 | 86.4 |
| Teo's wage from the drum | 17 | 17 |
| Anselm's shop | 8.5 | 8.5 |
| **Out:** the drum's levy for its paid posts (~7%) | 6.0 | 6.0 |
| Personal compute (Anselm 8, Teo 10, Wren 10, Ossian 6) | 34 | 24 (Wren dark) |
| Food beyond the Mill-table credit | 7 | 7 |
| Home: appliances, robots, devices, lamps | 10.8 | 10.8 |
| Other people's time | 9 | 9 |
| Into the goods account | 16 | 16 |
| Into the travel account | 10 | 20 (Wren's saving) |
| Banked | 19.1 | 19.1 |

- **Food:** the three eldest eat the Vat table at home; the children eat the Mill table at school.
- **Other people's time:** Ossian's after-school minder, Wren's flute teacher, haircuts and the odd dinner cooked by someone else, at ~3 kWh an hour.
- **Goods:** ~16 kWh a day is ~230 kg of new things a year for four, about the 2-kW average, with two of the four still growing.
- **Energy:** of ~112 kWh a day coming in, ~78 goes on energy. The rest is time paid to other people, and savings.

**Rights.** Each place in Sorrel carries one birth right, so a couple has two children; at a death the place goes back to the drum's pool and a child is born into it (economy.md). Anselm and Teo spent theirs on Wren and Ossian. Sorrel lets a right be given but not sold. Ilka, across Pear Street, gave hers to her sister in Chervil, and her nephew calls her his second mother.

## Day one: a working day
*Wren's third dark day. Nine days before the Window.*

**06:30.** The lamps come up over twenty minutes, warm and then white. Wren sits by the light panel in the kitchen for the twenty minutes at 2,500 lux her school insists on, which cost about a hundredth of a kWh. Usually she'd spend them in her worlds. Instead she runs the fingering for the ensemble's new piece without blowing a note. Her flute is about 1.2 m long, nearly twice the length of the flutes in old pictures: sound runs ~1.8 times faster here, so a pipe has to be that much longer to sing the same note.

**07:00.** Breakfast. Teo slices the Lovage sourdough and Anselm scrambles vat egg. Ossian wants jook, because he likes the chili crisp. The tea water comes from the 65 °C tap and boils in a minute. Everything goes on the table under lids, since heliox draws the heat out of food fast, and Sorrelers think an uncovered dish says something about your upbringing. Ossian knocks his cup off with an elbow. It lands a little antispinward of where it should have, and he says "anti", the way children in Sorrel do, as though the drum had done it. Breakfast for four costs about 5 kWh, most of it covered by the commons' food credit.

**07:20.** Teo goes down. A cab from the core at the end of Pear Street drops her 76 decks in about thirty seconds, its cabin leaning a little against the Coriolis push and her weight creeping up from 0.9 g to a full g, then sidesteps along the Keel and opens onto the Quaysides at Station Ring 3.

The Quaysides are the busiest blocks in Sorrel: markets, freight, and the platforms where glide trains come in through the jacket. The Gate is a long room off the inbound platform, with clean benches, a sequencer bay and a cold room. Most of what comes through is produce from neighbouring drums' racks, Lovage's cherry tomatoes and Tansy's strawberries, since produce is the one food worth moving between drums. Every crate arrives sealed and certified, and Teo spot-checks one in fifty.

At ten an old man from Rue comes through holding a flask in both hands. He has swapped homes to live near his daughter, and in the flask is his late wife's yogurt culture, which she kept alive for sixty years. He hasn't registered it.

"I can pull its genome from Rue's library and grow you a fresh one within a week," Teo says. "It'll be the same yogurt."

"It won't be hers."

She takes a sample and runs it while he waits, and the sequencer finds what she half expected: a prophage, a dormant virus folded into the bacterium's own genome. In his kitchen it's harmless. In one of Sorrel's dairy vats, if it woke, it could take a line down. The rules say hold it or destroy it. Teo calls the strain library, and they arrange the gentle version. The original goes into the library's isolation vault, where he can visit it, and the library cures a copy of the prophage, which takes about a week. Until then she gives him a jar of the library's own yogurt. "Next week," she tells him, "you'll have hers."

**09:00.** Anselm opens Dace's: a long room with a cutting table, a sewing machine, a small fabricator, a lathe, and racks of offcuts sorted by fibre and colour. The morning brings:
- A woman with her grandfather's coat, to be relined in this season's colour, the hard algae blue that half of Ashgrove's linings have turned this year. The dye weighs nothing. The lining is ~300 g of spun cellulose, about 5 kWh, and the work is two hours. Fashion in Sorrel lives in linings, collars, cuffs and alterations, because that's where it costs nothing.
- A father with his son's trousers to be let out. Children's clothes in Sorrel are cut with a hand's width folded into every seam, and a good pair is let out three times before it's handed down.
- A young couple asking about a sofa. They're marrying, and new furniture is what a marriage gets. Anselm does the sums on the counter. A new sofa is ~40 kg, about 1,000 kWh, forty-six days of one dividend. Their grandparents' sofa, remade, needs ~4 kg of new webbing, filling and cover (~100 kWh) and about forty hours of his time (~120). They take the remake. He doesn't tell them most couples do.

In the back of the shop is a chair under a sheet: his grandfather's, 110 years old, re-sprung and re-glued, with new feet turned on the lathe from offcuts and a cover of olive cloth that cost 12 kWh. It's for Mira's wedding in Lovage in six days. Mira is Teo's niece.

**10:30.** At the school on deck 75, break is spinball in the long hall. Every child in Sorrel learns its rule before they can read: *axis flies true, spin sinks, anti floats.* A ball thrown along the axis goes straight. Thrown spinward as hard as a child can throw it, it gains about a sixth of its weight and dives; thrown antispinward it loses as much and sails. Every throw breaks according to its heading, so the game is all angles. Ossian is good at it, because he's small and throws anti.

Lunch is the Mill table, from the commons: glass noodles and jelly cubes in chili oil and black vinegar, with the Nine shaken over the top, the bitter essential amino acids masked with salt and glutamate. Children treat it as a seasoning and adults as a joke. "Have some Nine" is what Sorrel says to anyone who looks peaky.

**15:30.** After school Wren goes to rehearsal in the Hall's back room. Anyone in Sorrel could listen to a perfect recording; Ashgrove still turns out to hear twelve teenagers play, because live music, like a coat cut by hand, is one of the things people here pay for. Afterwards she goes to the baths with three friends who are also dark. Going dark is a fast, and like any fast it has a sign: a black thread round the left wrist, meaning *don't message me, I won't see it*. The dark kids go to the baths because the baths are free, warm and full of people, and because the steam is where the drum's gossip lives.

**18:30.** Dinner: a pot of red stew, pulled mycelium in a palm-style sauce with a few grams of cultivated beef fat so it reads as beef, over Mill rice, with the Lovage bread, a side salad cut into four careful portions, and kuih lapis after. The salad was a few mouthfuls each and cost a third as much as the whole pot of rice. Anselm tells Ossian, as he does whenever there's mycelium, about the orange-steak year, when a blight took out Sorrel's Fusarium line and the drum ate Neurospora for a season, and the steaks came out pinkish-orange and tasting of nuts. Sorrel still cooks orange steaks on the anniversary. Dinner for four costs about 9 kWh, ~4 of it more than the Mill table would have.

Teo tells them about the yogurt man, without his name.

**20:00.** Anselm spends two hours in a shared world with three friends from his apprenticeship, who live in Tansy, Rue and a drum four rows over; they're all within a few hundred kilometres, so the world runs without lag. Teo reads. Ossian does half an hour with his tutor, which is mostly patient. Wren practises in her room, which shares a wall with Ilka's, and Ilka knocks twice, which means *lovely, now stop*.

**23:00.** The lamps fade. Anselm sets the laundry for one in the morning, when kWh are cheapest. Across Sorrel the kitchens shut and the evening worlds wind down, and the machines take the lamp-night: the raft's batch work and the Yards run harder, and the slow vat lines turn over. The Daces have spent ~42 kWh on the day itself. The rest has gone to their accounts and to other people.

## Day two: the Hollow
*The next day. Ashgrove's turn.*

The Hollow holds about 1.4 million people at the density of a crowded beach, under 1% of Sorrel, so the drum shares it out. It's open sixteen hours a day, and each deck gets a six-hour turn about every eight weeks, which works out to the three-quarters of an hour a week the Hollow can give everyone. Sorrel's elders get more: fixed goods here go by age, so the old have the lake every morning at lamp-dawn. Elsewhere on Herb Row, that's the main thing people know about Sorrel.

Today Ashgrove has the bright half, nine to three. The Daces take a cab up 284 decks to Hedda's, a little over a minute, and lose 40% of their weight on the way, the cabin leaning further as it climbs. Ossian is bouncing on his toes before the doors open, and on deck 361 he covers the corridor to her door in four long floating strides.

Hedda's flat is small, and it's the brightest place the children know. She spends almost nothing on compute and about a tenth of her dividend on light: a 4 m² garden along one wall, lit like an overcast day for twelve hours (~1.9 kWh a day), with a tomato vine in it that she talks to. A bright home is the plainest sign of wealth in the Shade. In an equal-dividend drum it's also a sign of what someone has decided to go without, and Hedda likes to point out that it's the drum's radiators, not her wallet, that stop her having a bigger one. Today she cuts the reddest tomato and wraps it in a cloth.

From deck 361 it's fifteen decks to the Hollow's floor, about fourteen seconds.

You come up into the Hollow through the ground, out of a cab pavilion among trees, and the first thing everyone does is look up and keep looking. The land rises on both sides of you and keeps rising until it's overhead. The far bank hangs three kilometres above you, upside down, and because filtered heliox carries no dust and scatters almost no light, it's perfectly sharp: you can see people on it, and a child over there flying a kite back at you. Along the axis, a kilometre and a half up, the sunline has finished its three-hour dawn. By nine it's too bright to look at, and the Hollow lies under 100,000 lux, a clear noon on Earth, for six hours; then come three hours of dusk. Lovage holds its noon for twelve. Sorrel thinks that's showing off.

The floor is the innermost deck: 9.4 km around and 3.75 km long, thirty-five square kilometres at half a g, under a fifth of a square metre for each person in Sorrel. There are lawns, and the drum's orchards, whose trees get their light free; a lake that holds part of the drum's water reserve; and at either end the terraced end walls, climbing toward the axis and getting lighter with every tier, until the top terraces are gardens where a child can jump the height of a house.

**The Spindle.** Ossian and Wren take the Spindle lift up the back wall to the axis. Within seventy-five metres of it you weigh under 3% of what you would at the rim: flying country. At full sun the middle of the Spindle is closed, since a few tens of metres from the sunline the light would burn you. But the line stops about four hundred and fifty metres short of each end wall, and the flying halls are there, in a soft half-light around the axis, inside a long sock of netting. You hire wing-sleeves at the gate, fabric fins from wrist to hip, and swim through the air. Flying along the axis is easy. Flying across it is a lesson: Coriolis bends your path and you come out somewhere you didn't aim, which is why the nets are there and why everyone in Sorrel learns to fly lengthwise. In the lamp-night the whole Spindle opens, and people fly its length in the dark. Wren, who has left everything at home, flies for an hour without thinking about anything at all, and says later it was the best hour since she went dark.

**The lake.** Teo swims. At half a g the waves come in slow and tall, and a dive off the jetty takes half again as long to fall, so you have time to think about it. Anselm sleeps on the grass with his jacket over his eyes. Hedda sits on the elders' bench where she sits most mornings.

**The picnic** costs about 6 kWh for five and is mostly cheap things: pastries, hard cheese and crackers, a Mill-sugar cake, lactic sodas for the children, and gin and soda for the adults at about a fifth of a kWh a glass. The expensive item is Hedda's tomato, cut into five on the cloth. It's worth about half a kWh. Everyone eats their fifth slowly.

Ossian asks whether the sunline ever goes out.

"Once, for me," Hedda says. "The Eleven Days. I was nineteen."

She tells it the way she always does. Merrow's beam comes from a patch of the Lantern about eleven light-months away, so the power reaching the raft today left it eleven months ago. One day it simply stopped arriving. "Somewhere on the Lantern, the year before, a patch had failed. The gap had been coming toward us the whole time, and nobody could have warned us sooner, because a warning travels at the speed of light, same as the gap." The Lantern's message arrived forty minutes after the dark. It had been written eleven months earlier, by whoever watched the patch fail, and it said *about ten days*. It was eleven.

All of Merrow dropped to its survival load: air handling, scrubbers and coolant pumps, about a twentieth of the usual power, from fuel cells burning shield hydrogen against banked oxygen. No lamps, no kitchens, no worlds. The cabs crawled, two trips a day each, with the riders going down paying for the riders going up, and for anything else people walked the stairs. "Two hundred million people in Sorrel, and the only light was whatever people's devices had in them." The food plant stopped, so nothing was making new air, and the air got very slightly thinner each day. Everyone ate their larder. The Glide kept running, because it needs no power, so the drums along every row passed spare food and parts to each other right through it. And Merrow braked its cyclers a little at every station, so the trains gave their speed back as electricity. "We ran slow trains then. Braking every one of them bought us three days with the air plant running, right in the middle. You could feel the air get better."

"Was it really a patch?" Wren asks.

"That's what the Lantern said," says Hedda. "Some people believed it." A deliberate cut has to have its length set when it's sent, since anything the raft says back takes the best part of two years to end it: it's a sentence, not a siege. Eleven days would have been a short one.

Afterwards Merrow voted to run its cycler trunks fast, at 10 to 20 km/s, so the trains now hold months of air and food instead of days. "It wouldn't save us from a real cut," Hedda says. "It'd save us from a short one."

Wren works it out halfway through a pastry: that's why there are express cyclers. "So I'm riding on the Eleven Days."

"Everyone is," says Hedda.

At three the sunline begins to ease toward evening, and the Hollow trades Ashgrove for some other deck. In the cab down Hedda reminds Ossian that the Window is eight days away, and asks whether he still wants her to take him. He looks at her as though she's said something stupid.

## Day three: Lovage
*Five days later. Wren's ninth dark day.*

Hedda doesn't come. A glide ends in a catch: the quay's cradle takes the car on a soft arm and brings a full g back over a second or two, and at 93 she'd rather not. "The catch is a young people's ride," she says, and sends a twist of paper with seeds from her tomato instead.

Dressing takes the morning, because in Sorrel your wedding clothes are the clothes you have, altered. Teo's grey dress has been re-dyed a deep green, which cost less than a cup of coffee. Anselm wears his father's jacket. Ossian's trousers have been let out a second time. Wren wears a dress borrowed from one of the dark kids, and her black thread, which she knows her Lovage cousins will tease her about.

The chair goes in a padded bag and the gin in another: two bottles for each adult, which is what Lovage lets a visitor bring in without duty. In Sorrel a bottle of gin costs about 2.8 kWh, less than an apple. In Lovage, after duty, it costs about what a new pair of shoes does, and Lovage has the bootleggers to show for it. Last year four people there died of spirit cut with the food plant's methanol, and the warnings are posted on every platform of its Quaysides. Every Sorreler at a Lovage wedding brings their two bottles, and Lovage pretends not to look forward to it.

**The glide.** They go down 76 decks to Station Ring 3 and through the Quaysides, where Teo waves to the Gate's night crew going off shift, and onto the platform, where a glide train of a few hundred seats waits at the inner face of the jacket. The cars are squat and heavily armoured. The dark hall outside is flooded with the Lantern's hard X-rays, enough to kill an unshielded person within minutes, so the cars carry thick shielding on every side that faces it, and their windows face the one safe way, aft. Mass costs nothing on the Glide, so nobody minds the weight.

The train passes out through the jacket, fifty metres of liquid hydrogen at twenty kelvin, in a short dark tunnel, onto a cradle on the quay, the band of track round the drum's waist. The cradle carries it round toward the lane's touch point. Ossian counts down. At the touch point the cradle lets go, the weight goes out of everyone in about a second, and they're falling in a straight line at 174 metres a second, toward Lovage.

Two minutes and twenty seconds. Through the aft windows is the only view of outside most people in Sorrel ever get. Mostly it's dark: a hall nearly four kilometres deep, between the rectennas in front and the radiators behind, with the radiator layer running away like a ceiling over the world. What you see are lights. Every drum wears a ring of quay lights round its waist, and the drums stand in rows 24.5 km apart, so the rings go back rank behind rank to a vanishing point, each one a drum six kilometres across and nearly four tall, with two hundred million people inside. Behind the train Sorrel's ring shrinks; ahead Lovage's grows. Ossian keeps his face against the window the whole way. Children are told, accurately, that the hall would kill them, and they still want to see it.

Then the catch: a cradle waiting at Lovage's touch point, the soft arm, and over a second or two the weight comes back. Ossian laughs. Teo's stomach turns over, as it has for twenty-two years. They go in through Lovage's jacket to its rim deck, where Lovage's Gate checks Hedda's seeds. Teo watches them do it, professionally, and approves.

**Lovage** is brighter. Its arcades are lit higher, its shop windows hold new things, and its people wear newer clothes. Because it auctions its high decks, Lovage's money lives up, and the lift lobbies get grander as you climb. Teo grew up near the bottom. To Sorrel ears, Lovage people always sound as though they're about to laugh.

**The wedding** is on a terrace of Lovage's back end wall, at a third of a g, with the whole bright Hollow curving away below and above, and Lovage's sunline still at full noon at six in the evening. The vows are short. Then the officiant cuts an orchard apple crosswise, the dearest food at the wedding, and holds up the halves so everyone can see the five-pointed star of seeds at the core. Lovage has no Windows, and for most people in Lovage it's the only star they will ever see. Mira and Tobiah eat a half each.

"That's not a real star," Ossian says, not quietly.

"Ask your gran," says Anselm.

**The gifts.** Lovage relatives give Hollow hours, bought at Lovage's auction, the classic gift. Tobiah's family gives a new sofa: 40 kg, ~1,000 kWh, pooled from twenty-two relatives over a year, which in Lovage is the proper thing and is meant to be seen. Tobiah is a third child, which in Lovage means his family bought a birth right, and Teo, who grew up here, notices how many of the guests know it. The Daces give the chair. Anselm tells its story in three sentences: his grandfather's, 110 years old, remade with his own hands. Some of the Lovage aunts look at it the way Lovage looks at old things. Mira sits in it, and stays sitting in it for a while.

The gin goes onto the table and doesn't stay there long. Teo's brother Bram, the bride's father, hugs her for a long time.

**Dancing** at a third of a g is mostly leaping, and every leap comes down about a hand's width spinward of where it began, so Lovage's young people have a reel that works its way round the terrace a hand's width at a time. Wren knows it from a world and dances it all night, black thread and all.

**Home.** The last glide back is at one in the morning. Ossian falls asleep before the release and stays asleep through a hundred and forty weightless seconds, floating against his harness, and through the catch.

## Day four: the Window
*Three days later. Wren's twelfth dark day.*

Every child in Sorrel goes to the Window once, at about eight, with one adult of their choosing. Sorrel keeps a dozen, behind the drum, each a long low gallery seating about a hundred and fifty, and between them they take the drum's eight-year-olds through nearly a thousand at a time. Ossian chose his grandmother when he was four.

The Windows are outside the spinning world, and you leave it through the back axle, at the far end of the Spindle, where the drum's heat goes out. Ossian and Hedda ride the Spindle lift up the Hollow's back wall, past the terraces, and the weight drains out of them. In the last few hundred metres the cabin swings until down points sideways, then toward the axis, then nowhere at all, and by the top he's holding her sleeve so as not to drift away. At 93 she moves through zero g better than he does. "Forty years," she says.

The axle hall is the loudest place Ossian has ever been. Pipes as wide as rooms carry the drum's heat out as ammonia, ~220 cubic metres a second, a river's worth, through the great rotary joint into the still part of the raft. A guide meets them, a young woman who knows Hedda's name. They float into a chamber that turns with Sorrel, and slowly it stops turning, or rather the world around it does: for a little under two minutes Ossian watches the whole hall wheel round him once, and then they're in the part of the raft where nothing turns. A short armoured car takes them along a shielded track out through the radiator layer to the gallery.

Inside it's dim and red, with couches in tiers facing a long shutter and seventy-odd other children with their adults, whispering. The guide makes everyone wait twenty minutes in the red light, because eyes need that long to wake up to the dark, and nobody wants to waste their one time. Hedda spends it telling Ossian what she did out here for forty years: chasing ammonia leaks along the panel rows on a sled, changing pumps, patching punctures, in a suit whose visor looked aft.

Then the shutter opens.

There's no near star at all. The cluster of stars that once crowded the middle of the galaxy around the hole was taken apart more than a hundred million years ago; its hydrogen is in the drums' jackets and the hole's fuel. What fills the Window instead is what the back crews call the River: a broad band of stars right across the sky, more than anyone could count, pale gold, the brightest of them orange, thick and bright in the middle and frayed at the edges, with dark rifts of dust lying across it. Above and below it hangs a fainter glow the crews call the Shelf. The River is the nuclear stellar disk, a hundred to two hundred parsecs out, centuries away by light, and the Shelf is the bulge beyond it.

And here and there, set into the sky, are places where nothing shines at all: black shapes, from specks to one about the width of a fingernail at arm's length. "Those are the holes," Hedda says. "Rafts. Some about our size, some a hundred times bigger." About a sixth of the sky is rafts.

"Watch the edge of the big one," she says, "by that red star." They watch. It takes most of an hour, and Ossian nearly misses it, but the edge of the raft creeps over the star and the star goes out. "We used to bet on those," says Hedda.

He asks where the metal comes from, because his class has done this, and she points at the River. "Out there. They take the stars apart and sort them, and send the metal in. It falls for about a million years." She taps the couch, his sleeve, his chest. "Your chair. Your clothes. Every bit of you heavier than helium. All of it fell out of that."

Then he asks about the apple.

"The apple," she says, "is a picture of this."

They stay the full hour. On the way back, in the part of the raft where nothing turns, she gives him her crew patch, a square of cloth with a band of tiny stitched stars across it, older than his father.

**Home.** Everyone wants to know what it was like, and Ossian can't say.

At midnight Wren's twelfth dark day ends, with ~120 kWh saved in her travel account. That pays for the express cycler to Juno's drum, a little under sixteen hours at 20 km/s for ~111 kWh, and the slow line home: six days at 2 km/s for about 1 kWh, in a berth, eating in a dining car with strangers, which is how people in the Shade cross great distances when they have more time than kWh. She books both. There are about 8 kWh left over, and in the morning she'll buy her grandmother an apple. She takes off the black thread, switches her worlds back on, sends Juno a message, and waits seven seconds.

Ossian has asked for his lamp to be turned all the way off, which nobody in Sorrel does. He lies on his back holding the patch, in a dark as complete as anyone in the drum has seen since the Eleven Days, and waits for his eyes to wake up. Around him the drum hums: fans, pumps, two hundred million people asleep, the lamp-night's machines at work. He knows exactly where the River is now. It's past the end of the drum and behind the radiators, a hundred parsecs away, and everything he has ever touched fell out of it.

## What the sketch assumes
**Choices among options the docs leave open:**
- **Fixed goods and birth rights** (economy.md's tables). Sorrel allots high decks and Hollow hours by age, and birth rights are giftable only. Lovage auctions its fixed goods, spends the rents on its commons, and lets rights be sold.
- **Wages** as in economy.md: the drum pays its posts from a ~7% levy, and private work runs at ~3 kWh an hour. Sorrel puts half a child's dividend in their own name at fourteen.
- **Hollow time** rationed by deck: a six-hour turn about every eight weeks, plus daily lamp-dawn hours at the lake for Sorrel's elders.
- **Naming:** children named for the dead whose places they take (economy.md's proposal), with the drum's register telling families whose place it was.

**New here:**
- **Merrow:** a 0.1 AU raft in mid-Shade, its beam patch ~0.27 pc away (~11 light-months, economy.md's mid-Shade case). Herb Row: a run of drums founded together.
- **The Court's light:** Ashgrove's ~53 W a head of park light (daily-life.md) lights its 5,400 m² Court to 10,000 lux for twelve hours with ~2,700 lux left for the terraces, or the Court and its terraces (~1.5 ha) to ~5,300 lux.
- **The Windows:** shielded galleries on the aft face of the radiator layer, reached through the back axle. Sorrel keeps a dozen and sends every child once; Lovage has none.
- **The Eleven Days:** a beam gap 74 years ago, with the Lantern's explanation arriving 40 minutes after it began. Slow cyclers at 2 km/s held ~0.1 GJ a resident (raft-transport.md), ~2.6 days of full air and food. Merrow then ran its trunks at 10–20 km/s (~3–6 GJ a resident, ~2½–5 months).
- **The sky from the back of a raft:** the nuclear star cluster's stars long since stripped (matter-delivery.md), and its white dwarfs mined or herded out of the Shade, so there are no near stars. Naked-eye stars are mostly the nuclear stellar disk's giants. Around a mid-Shade raft, rafts are ~13 AU apart on average and cover about a sixth of the outward sky; the nearest, ~7 AU off at ~200 km/s, crosses stars at ~2 arcminutes an hour.
- **Smaller customs:** "kays"; the black thread for going dark; spinball; the growing seam; the apple cut crosswise at weddings; Knotnight and Booking Day; pears with a winter; Lovage's two-bottle allowance; laundry in the lamp-night, when kWh are cheapest (economy.md).

## Questions it raises
- **The sunline and the Spindle share the axis.** At full sun the line puts ~6.7 kW/m² of light on anything 75 m from it, six or seven times Earth's noon sunshine. Settled in raft-habitat.md: the lit line stops ~450 m short of each end wall, with flying halls in the gaps, and the rest of the Spindle opens in the lamp-night.
- **Glide catches and the frail.** Hedda doesn't glide. Is there a gentler catch lane, or do the old and the ill simply stay home? (raft-transport.md)
- **The cluster's white dwarfs** would wreck any raft they passed near, so the Shade must have cleared them, presumably by mining them first.
- **Spirits across the quay.** Sorrel's cheap gin, carried over in visitors' bags, may be part of what keeps Lovage's methanol deaths as low as they are.
- **Equal dividends and hard posts.** Do the Gate and the back crews fill on a wage, or do they need standing and perks on top?
- **Hollow time by age** gives Sorrel's elders several times an equal share: economy.md's "allotted by age or service" column at work. How much do Low-deck families mind?

## Method
- **Drum:** raft-habitat.md's nation drum, ω = 0.0572 rad/s (110 s a turn); deck n at r = 3,000 − 4(n − 1) m, g = r ÷ 3,000. Ashgrove: decks 73–78 (r = 2,712–2,692 m, 0.90 g). The flat on deck 77 (2,696 m, 0.90 g); Hedda's on deck 361 (1,560 m, 0.52 g); Ossian Hale's deck 286 (1,860 m, 0.62 g). In the 2 km drum first assumed, the same weights fell on decks 47–52, 51, 240 and 190.
- **Cabs** (drum-transport.md) at 20 m/s and 1.5 m/s², plus a second for jerk: deck 77 to the rim in ~30 s, to deck 361 in ~71 s; deck 361 to the Hollow floor in ~14 s. Spindle lifts climb the end walls at 12 m/s.
- **Hollow:** 35.3 km² ÷ 25 m² ≈ 1.4 million at once; open 16 hours a day, ~0.8 hours a week each (economy.md), so a six-hour turn every ~7.6 weeks.
- **Coriolis:** extra weight 2ωv, ±10% of local weight at 8 m/s on deck 77 and ±17% for a 13 m/s throw. A cup dropped from 1 m lands ~1.8 cm antispinward. A 2.5 m/s leap at 0.33 g lands ~11 cm spinward.
- **Sunline:** 100,000 lux at the floor, 1.5 km out, is ~330 W/m² of light (~300 lm per radiant watt). A line source falls off as 1/r: ~10 kW/m² at 50 m, ~6.7 kW/m² at 75 m, ~1.7 kW/m² at 300 m. raft-habitat.md's shortened line draws ~12 GW at full sun.
- **Park light:** 3,000 × 53 W ≈ 160 kW. The Court takes 108 kW (5,400 m² × 10,000 lux ÷ 250 lm/W, lit half the day), leaving ~50 kW for ~9,600 m² of terraces (~2,700 lux); spread over 1.5 ha, the whole share gives ~5,300 lux.
- **Hedda's garden:** 4 m² × 10,000 lux ÷ 250 lm/W × 12 h = 1.9 kWh a day.
- **Glide:** quay at 3,050 m, 174.4 m/s; a 24.5 km hop in 140 s.
- **Windows:** a drum of 200 M sees ~1,400–6,800 births a day at lives of 400 to 80 years (economy.md), so ~4,500 eight-year-olds a day at 120. A dozen galleries take ~900 children and their adults a round, five rounds a day.
- **Cyclers:** 1 g ramps of 20,400 km (34 minutes) at each end, so a million km at 20 km/s takes ~14.5 h plus glides and boarding. Capsule 200 kg at 1% loss: 111 kWh at 20 km/s, 1.1 kWh at 2 km/s (5.8 days). Light delay over a million km: 3.3 s one way.
- **Rafts:** 1.27 × 10³³ people ÷ 6 × 10¹⁹ per raft ≈ 2 × 10¹³ rafts in the 0.9–1.25 pc shell, ~13 AU apart; nearest neighbour ~0.55 × spacing ≈ 7 AU, where a 0.1 AU raft is 0.8° across.
- **Giants:** L = 100–1,000 L☉ with bolometric corrections of −0.8 to −1.5 gives M_V ≈ +0.5 to −1.3, so V ≈ 3.7–7 at 100–200 pc, before dust.
- **Heliox flute:** a concert flute's ~0.67 m × 1.8 ≈ 1.2 m.
- **Household:** dividends 4 × 21.6; Teo full time at economy.md's ~6,200 kWh a year (~17 a day); Anselm ~1,000 hours at ~3 kWh. Energy lines from daily-life.md's wallet: compute, Vat-table premium, appliances, devices and home light, goods (~25 kWh/kg) and travel.
