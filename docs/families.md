# Families

## Summary
- **The Shade wants more children than it can have.** About one couple in twenty has no children, so the families with a third replace them, and wanting a third pays by at least the room they leave (The floor under the wish). Nothing a drum can live with removes that, so within a few thousand years of any start most couples want a third. The Shade is far older than that and every new drum is founded by an old one's people, so there is no drum where it hasn't happened.
- **So drums don't hold the wish. They hold who is born to a place.** A household's allowance is two children by right and a third by leave. Leave comes out of the room the childless and the early dead leave, about one birth in eighteen: enough for one family in eight.
- **A child born outside the allowance is placeless-born.** It is registered, proofread, fed and schooled like any child, and grows up in its parents' home. What it lacks is a claim: it can't inherit a place, be named heir to one or bring children into one, unless it is placed. Earth's land-short societies held their numbers the same way. They never limited births; they limited heirs.
- **It holds because the cost is the child's standing, which no wallet softens.** A household that waits keeps its chance of a third by leave, about one in eight. One that has a child outside gives that up and gains only the chance that the child is placed. So waiting pays wherever the door is narrower than the draw's odds, for rich families and poor ones alike, and over the Shade's age selection has made people want children who can have a place.
- **Drums differ in how leave is given and in how hard the line is drawn.** Leave goes by lot (the draw), by gift from the heirless, by sale, or by a block's say. The line is hard or soft on rented rooms, on a place that comes late, on the keep a household is charged, and on how wide the door is: adoption into an heirless place, a marriage the drum allows, long service. Every placing is a third somebody wasn't given.
- **A steady drum sits a few percent over par, for good.** With three births in a hundred outside the allowance and one in ten of those placed, a drum runs ~2.7% over par, about one person in forty is placeless-born, and one family in nine is given a third. Where its houses hold about one place in fifty for them, it sits near par with that many fewer families.
- **Their poverty does no work.** The line is drawn in householders, not kWh, so how well a drum keeps its placeless-born moves neither its numbers nor the wish. In most drums they are shareless for life: full-time work pays ~80% of a wallet from eighteen, and old age rests on a house's box, on kin or on savings. A few drums lend them the dividends of shares that are waiting for a child (What the placeless-born live on).
- **The sore is the line outside.** The placeless-born have no place to lose, so the rule that holds households doesn't hold them. Most have no children. Mixed in with everyone else, the few who do stay a trace. Where they become a people apart, marrying their own and raising their children in the habit, their line takes a tenth of a drum's births in ~500–1,500 years. Drums keep the placeless-born mixed in, place their children, charge a keep or fit them, and each answer costs something.
- **Counting is what happens when the line fails.** A drum that votes its line away has three children a couple within a generation. A drum well over par that cuts the allowance below two finds that people will wait for a third and won't wait for a first, and has to fit (people.md § Counted drums).
- **How a household passes still differs:** whole to one heir (canon's stem places), to one heir with portions for the rest, split among all the children, or kept by the siblings together for life. It decides who carries a third and what families are like.

## Where the Shade is
- **The clock ran out long ago.** From a fresh spread of wishes, half of couples want a third after ~1,500–3,800 years under a draw and four in five or more after ~10,000; where custom alone held births it went faster (The floor under the wish). The Shade has stood closed, at fixed numbers, for far longer than that: Sorrel's Register alone runs back ~23,000 years, and the growth horizon is 1–2 Gyr.
- **No drum starts fresh.** New drums are founded at the rafts' edges by old drums' placeless (economy.md § The frontier). A raft's drums grow by ~1.4–3.6 × 10⁻⁹ a year, so only one to four drums in 100,000 are under 10,000 years old, and their founders brought the old wish with them.
- **What saturated means.** Most couples in every drum want a third, and would have a fourth. The wish is no longer something a rule can turn up or down. A drum can only decide which children have a place, and what selection still works on is narrower: whatever gets a child one.
- **How old it has to be.** Saturation takes a few thousand years. A small leak takes either the habit people brought with them from Earth or, where that was ever lost, some tens of thousands of years of narrow doors (The leak). people.md leaves the Shade's age at story time open; this doc assumes it is well past both, as Sorrel's Register suggests.
- **What the climbs are now.** The first Counts and climbing wishes of the 6 Nov models describe the Shade's first ten thousand years. They are not a cross-section of the Shade today. What a drum faces now if it takes its line away is faster: three children a couple at once, and more where nothing stops at three (Loosening and tightening).

## Born to a place
### The allowance
- **Two by right, a third by leave (proposal).** Every household with a place may have two children. A third needs leave, and leave is issued only as fast as there is room.
- **Room.** One couple in twenty has no children and a few people die before 35, so two children a household leave about one slot in eighteen unfilled (5.7% of a drum's births). That is the drum's room: ~95,000 children a year in a nation drum, a third child for one family in eight.
- **Whose room it is.** In a holdings drum room has an address. A household that ends without a child leaves a place; the place needs an heir, and the heir a spouse, so it makes room for two. The household names its heir, usually a child not yet born, three generations down a brother's or sister's line, and the naming is that child's leave. The second child, who will marry in somewhere, is the drum's to give: by the draw, by sale or by the block (How leave is given). A child who dies young is replaced by right.
- **Born to a place.** A child born inside the allowance is born to a place. It can inherit one, be named heir to one or marry into one, and its own first two children will be born to a place in turn. The clinic records it at the birth and the drum's register carries it beside the name.
- **Both parents.** A household's allowance needs both partners born to a place, or placed. A marriage across the line is a marriage, but it brings no children into the place until the partner from outside is placed. Athens asked the same of its citizens (Earth anchors).
- **Later place.** A couple who were both born to a place, but whose place hasn't come yet, may have a child while they wait. In most drums the child takes its place when the place comes, as canon law made a child legitimate by its parents' later marriage. It costs no room, since the child is one of the household's two. Strict drums refuse, as English law did for seven centuries (Earth anchors).
- **Medicine sets the room.** Room is whatever a drum's childless leave. Selection has long since thinned people who want no children, as it thins anything that leaves fewer descendants, so canon's one couple in twenty are mostly people who wanted children and didn't have them: never paired, parted or widowed early, a place that came too late, a house entered young, what the clinics can't mend. A drum with better clinics and better luck has less room and a narrower door:

| Couples childless | Room, of births | A third for one family in | A household's odds of leave |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 in 12 | 8.6% | 5.5 | 18% |
| 1 in 20 (canon) | 5.7% | 8.8 | 11% |
| 1 in 33 | 3.7% | 14 | 7% |
| 1 in 50 | 2.7% | 20 | 5% |
| 1 in 100 | 1.7% | 35 | 3% |

  (With 3% of births outside the allowance and one in ten of those placed. A drum whose clinics improve has less room at once, and for a generation more children than slots.)

### The placeless-born
- **What they have.** A register entry, proofreading, the commons, school, their parents' home while they grow, the right of the Glide and the right to marry. A placeless-born child is nobody's secret, and is a different thing from a wild-born one (people.md), which is born outside the clinics.
- **What they lack.** A claim on any place. The line rule passes over them, and children born to them are placeless-born too. In most drums they also stand last in the family's line for a share, behind every child born to a place, so most of them work for their wallets (economy.md § Children).
- **Who they are.** Mostly a household's third, born without leave and raised beside a brother and a sister who were born to a place. Some are the children of couples whose place never came. A few are the children of the placeless-born.
- **Their lives.** The houses and the lent flats are theirs first (people.md § The placeless): a home, a calling, companions, and no nursery. They are most of a steady drum's placeless, and many of the people it pays for their time: cooks, tutors, nurses, back crews.

### Placing
- **The door (proposal).** A placeless-born person who is placed counts from then on as born to a place. There are four ways in. **Adoption:** an heirless household names a living adult as its heir instead of a child unborn, as Japanese houses adopted the ablest son-in-law. **Marriage:** to someone born to a place, with the drum's or the block's leave; where leave is drawn, the couple enters the same draw as couples seeking a third, at the same odds (Marriage across the line). **Service:** some drums grant a placing for decades on the back crews or in an office of trust, as Rome gave its auxiliaries citizenship and lawful marriage after twenty-five years; the crews elect whom they take, since hardly anyone dies in service and a placing for every volunteer would be a wide door (Service). **A later place,** for a child whose parents' place came after it did.
- **Every placing spends room.** A placed person fills a slot a third would have filled, so the door and the draw come out of one account. With three births in a hundred outside the allowance, placing half of them takes thirds from one family in eight to one in eleven; placing all of them, to one in eighteen.
- **Founding is no door.** New drums are founded by the placeless (economy.md § The frontier), but a standing drum sees one founder's share about every three and a half years, against ~50,000 children born outside its allowance each year.

### Why it holds
- **What a household weighs,** counted in children who will head households. A household that keeps to its allowance leaves two, plus its chance of a third by leave, about 0.12. One that has a child outside leaves two, plus the chance that child is placed, and in most drums it is out of the draw for good. So the door decides it:

| Of the placeless-born, placed | A household gains by having a child outside | What the habit of doing so does |
|---|---|---|
| None | −0.12 of a child | Halves in ~9,000 years or more, down to accidents |
| 1 in 10 | −0.02 | Holds level |
| 1 in 4 | +0.13 | Doubles in ~6,000 years or more |
| Half | +0.39 | Doubles in ~2,000–5,000 years |
| All | +0.91 | Doubles in ~900–2,200 years; placings take all the room in ~2,000 |

- **The door can be no wider than the draw.** A drum can place about as large a share of its placeless-born as the share of its households it gives a third, one in eight, and no more. Past that, having a child outside and hoping is a better bet than waiting, and the leak grows until placings have eaten the room.
- **The forfeit matters as much.** If a household with a child outside keeps its place in the draw, having one costs nothing even with the door shut, and pays with it ajar (+0.10 of a child at one in ten placed). And the draw's room can't go to placing a child of the household that wins it, or every household would have its third first and enter after.
- **Why a price never did this.** A charge in kWh is paid by whoever can afford it, so a priced third is a comfortable family's, and the poor family's third is born anyway. A child's standing costs every family the same, and it is paid in the one thing selection counts: grandchildren with households. Where leave is sold, a household that can't pay has no odds to lose, and for it any door pays.
- **What selection has made of people (proposal).** Selection counts children who head households (people.md § What births do to the gene pool). In drums where a child outside the allowance is a line that ends, it has favoured, over the Shade's age, people who want children they can place, and who would no more have one outside than their ancestors would have had one with no farm to leave it. People ache for a third and mostly don't want one born outside. Like waiting for a home first, it is felt as a wish, not obeyed as a law.

### The leak
- **A few births in a hundred (proposal).** Some children are still born outside the allowance: accidents, couples who stopped waiting, people who don't care, and the children of the placeless-born. England's parish registers show 1–7% of births outside marriage from the 1500s to the 1950s, under a rule of the same shape. Canon takes ~3% for a steady drum and one in ten placed.
- **Why it is small, and what a large one does.** People came to the Shade already wanting children they could place: under England's rule about nineteen births in twenty were inside it, without contraception. And a large leak mends itself, slowly. Where many households have a child outside, the few who wait have the room to themselves: with four households in five doing it, a waiting household's chance of a third is three in five. From there the habit falls to one household in five in ~3,000–7,000 years with the door shut and to one in twenty in ~8,000–19,000, and about six times more slowly with one in ten placed. So a line newly drawn in a drum that has lost the habit of keeping one leaks for thousands of years, and canon's 3% belongs to a Shade well past its first lines.
- **What a drum carries.** The placeless-born who aren't placed are extra lifetimes over par:

| Births outside the allowance | Placed | A third by leave for one family in | Drum sits over par | Placeless-born, of all people |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1% | 1 in 10 | 8.5 | 0.9% | 1 in 110 |
| 3% | None | 8.3 | 3.0% | 1 in 34 |
| 3% (canon) | 1 in 10 | 8.8 | 2.7% | 1 in 38 |
| 3% | Half | 11.3 | 1.5% | 1 in 68 |
| 3% | All | 17.7 | 0 | None |
| 5% | 1 in 10 | 9.1 | 4.5% | 1 in 23 |
| 8% | 1 in 10 | 9.7 | 7.2% | 1 in 15 |
| 8% | All | None left | 2.3% | 1 in 44 |

- **So a steady drum sits a few percent over par, for good.** A nation drum of 200 M shares holds ~205 M people, ~5 M of them placeless-born. Each year ~50,000 children are born outside the allowance and ~5,000 people are placed; ~90,000 thirds are born by leave. Each person's power is ~1.95 kWe. That is a drum whose placeless-born live in other households' rooms. Where houses hold places for them, the drum has fewer households and sits nearer par (Houses and par).

## The line outside
- **Nothing to lose.** The rule holds households because they have a chance of a third to forfeit. The placeless-born have no place, no draw, and a keep they often can't pay.
- **Most have no children anyway.** People want children once they have a home to pass on (people.md), and a lent flat or a house isn't one. Canon takes one placeless-born couple in twenty-five having children, three each, since nothing holds them to two (proposal).
- **The few who do are the next generation's parents.** Among the children of the placeless-born, the habit of having children outside is inherited from exactly the couples who had it. Mixed in with each generation's newly born outside, they stay a trace. Kept apart, they don't:

| How the placeless-born pair | Births to them, of a drum's births, after 3 / 10 / 20 generations | A tenth of a drum's births in |
|---|---|---|
| At random among all the placeless-born | 0.2% / 0.2% / 0.2% | Never |
| Half the children of the line pair within it | 0.2% / 0.2% / 0.2% | Never |
| The line keeps to itself | 0.2% / 0.2% / 0.2% | Never |
| The same, with the habit passed on strongly (b = 0.5) | 0.3% / 0.4% / 0.6% | ~1,500 years |
| The same, very strongly (b = 0.8) | 0.5% / 2.7% / 38% | ~500 years |
| Old lines marry only old lines | 0.2% / 0.3% / 0.7% | ~1,100 years (~760 at b = 0.5, ~1,800 at b = 0.2) |
| The same, half of each generation placed | 0.1% / 0.1% / 0.1% | Never |
| The same, couples outside held to two children | 0.1% / 0.1% / 0.1% | Never |
| Fitted until placed | None | Never |

  (b is how strongly the habit passes from parents to children: 0.35 unless shown. "Old lines marry only old lines" is the limit in which nothing dilutes a line.)
- **The threshold.** A line outside grows of itself once the share of its couples who have children, times half the children they have, times the share who aren't placed, passes 1. At three children and one in ten placed, that is three couples in four. Selection inside a line that keeps apart gets there. Dilution keeps a mixed one far below.
- **So shunning makes the caste.** A drum that keeps its placeless-born in its houses, halls and crews, marrying whom they like, has people who were born outside. A drum that pushes them into quarters of their own, or whose placeless-born have become a creed or a people, has a line, and within ~500–1,500 years the line is a tenth of its births and the drum is near the 10% over par at which drums have counted.
- **What drums do, none of it clean:**
  - **Keep them mixed in.** Houses that elect them, flats lent beside everyone else's, crews and halls that hire them, marriage with anyone. It is cheap, and it is the only answer that needs no force.
  - **Place the children of the line.** It ends a line in one generation and spends room, and a household that sees it done has one more reason to bet on the door.
  - **Charge the keep** (Where drums draw the line). The placeless-born who have children are mostly shareless and can't pay it, so it is worked off, and a drum that makes people work off their children has a house of correction.
  - **Fit the placeless-born until they are placed.** It ends the line outside for good, and it is counting one group (people.md § Counted drums). Drums that do it say they count nobody; their neighbours disagree.
  - **Do nothing,** and count everyone a thousand years on.
- **Earth's line outside drained away,** into towns, armies, colonies and early graves, and still it ran in families: Laslett argued that a few interrelated families lay behind much of an English parish's illegitimacy, generation after generation (others doubt they were so separate), and German guilds asked proof of legitimate birth of an apprentice, of a master and of a master's wife, since her children would carry the lack of it. The Shade has none of Earth's drains. It has the door, and the door can't be wide.

## What the placeless-born live on
Proposals, from working one such life through (third-chair.md; the sums are `models/placeless_life.py`). Figures are for a steady drawn-holdings drum of 200 M shares.

### Their poverty does no work
- **The line is drawn in householders, not kWh.** A child outside the allowance costs its household its chance of a third and gains it only the door (Why it holds). Nothing in that sum is a wallet. So how well a drum keeps its placeless-born moves neither its numbers nor the wish, and drums differ freely: shareless for life in one, a wallet in old age in the next, a house with a hall and a cook in a third.
- **One limit.** What a drum gives them can't look like a home to pass on, since that is the cue people have children on (people.md § What births do to the gene pool). A room for life, a box and a waiting wallet all end with the person.

### To eighteen
- **The same childhood, on a thinner wallet.** A placeless-born child has its parents' home, the school, the clinic and the Mill table. About one child in 33 is one. What differs is the household's page of the register: the entry beside the name, the keep, and the note that the household is out of the draw.

| The keep | kWh a day | Over eighteen years | The household's wallets, kWh a head a day |
|---|---|---|---|
| None (open drums) | 0 | 0 | 17.3 |
| The Mill-table credit, 0.23 kW | 5.5 | ~36,000 kWh, 4.6 wallet-years | 16.2 |
| Half the child's commons, 0.55 kW (Tansy) | 13.2 | ~87,000 kWh, 11 wallet-years | 14.6 |
| The whole commons, 1.1 kW | 26.4 | ~174,000 kWh, 22 wallet-years | 12.0 |

  (A family of four has 21.6 kWh a head.)
- **The brother and sister carry it too.** The keep comes out of four wallets, two of them the children's who were born to a place, for the first eighteen years of the third's life.
- **For a placeless-born couple it is most of a wage.** Two full-time wages are ~34 kWh a day, so one child's keep is 16%, 39% or 78% of them. At the top of the range it can only be worked off (The line outside).

### From eighteen
- **No dividend arrives.** A brother or sister born to a place has 21.6 kWh a day for life, working or not, and ~38.6 working full time. A placeless-born adult working full time has ~17: four-fifths of a wallet, and under half of what a working sibling has.
- **A home is a parent's place, a rented room or a house.** A room of 20 m² lets for ~2 kWh a day at par, an eighth of full-time pay (economy.md § Homes). The parents' place is theirs to live in while the parents live, which for a third child is until about eighty. Then it goes down its own line, to a great-nephew or great-niece in their twenties.

### Waiting shares
- **About 3% of a drum's shares are unheld at any time.** A share freed by a death waits in its family for the next child born there, and a child born ahead of a death waits for a share. people.md's family-tree model has ~3% of people shareless at par, nearly all of them children waiting ~6 years, and at par there are as many shares waiting as children: ~6 M in a nation drum.
- **Their dividends come to ~5.4 GW,** nearly what the drum's commons spends on all its placeless-born (~5.4 M × 1.1 kW ≈ 5.9 GW).
- **In most drums they fall to the commons.** The commons then comes to ~1.10 kW a head with the placeless-born counted in. It would be ~1.13 kW without them, so the neighbours do pay for a household's child outside, out of a surplus they would otherwise have had. That is what the keep recovers.
- **A few drums lend them to the old.** A placeless-born elder holds a waiting wallet: the dividend of a share between its death and its child, lent by the drum and moved to another waiting share when the child is born. Chervil does it (open-drums.md). English dower had the same shape: a widow's life interest in a third of her husband's land, which went back to his heir at her death. A waiting wallet for every placeless-born person over 85 takes about a quarter of the waiting shares and thins the commons by ~0.6%, and no child waits longer for a share.

### Old age
- **The gap.** At 120-year lives three people in ten are over 85. Someone born to a place has the dividend for life, and usually rents from the rooms of a place. A placeless-born person who stops work has the commons and nothing else, for twenty to thirty-five years.
- **Alone, it takes a third of a working life's pay.** In a drum that forbids interest, banking ~5 kWh a day from eighteen to eighty-five buys ~10 kWh a day after: a life on ~12 kWh a day while working and ~10 after, a little over half a wallet throughout. Stopping at seventy-five takes nearly half of pay, and at ninety-five a fifth. In a drum that lets interest run at ~3%, banking 6% of pay does it, and the old placeless are among its lenders.
- **The box.** A house keeps a common fund, as guilds and friendly societies kept a box: members pay in while they work and draw when they stop. Fifteen parts in a hundred of pay from election at about thirty to eighty-five pays ~4 kWh a day from then on, beside a room and a hall for life. A monastery's corrody was the same promise: bread, ale and a room for life, to a servant grown old or to a layman who had paid for it.
- **Kin.** When the parents die their shares pass over the placeless-born child to the family's waiting children, and their place to an heir three generations down. A placeless-born elder in a great-niece's back room is the unmarried aunt of every stem family on Earth, with the commons under her.

### Houses and par
- **About one place in fifty houses them all.** A drum of 200 M shares carries ~5.4 M placeless-born, ~4.1 M of them past their twenties. At a person's ~35 m² of home they fill ~1.86 M of its 90 M places.
- **Each place a house holds is a household that isn't there,** so the drum's births fall with its houses, and its numbers with them:

| Houses hold, of the drum's places | Households | People | Against par |
|---|---|---|---|
| None | 90.0 M | 205.4 M | +2.7% |
| 1% | 89.1 M | 203.3 M | +1.7% |
| 2.1% (enough to house them all) | 88.1 M | 201.1 M | +0.6% |
| 3% | 87.3 M | 199.2 M | −0.4% |

- **So "a few percent over par" and "houses" are two answers to one question.** A drum can carry its placeless-born as extra people in other households' rooms, with a commons 2.7% thinner, or house them and have 2% fewer families, for good. Open drums do the second with the Margin (open-drums.md); steady drums with houses.
- **Founding houses is slow, and emptying them is quick.** About 47,000 places a year end without an heir. A tenth of them given to houses builds the stock in ~390 years, and half in ~80, and each is a third some family wasn't given. Dissolved, the houses' places make room for ~3.7 M children at once, forty-two years of leave, and put ~4 M people into lodgings. Every assembly has heard the motion (Loosening and tightening); Tansy carried it once, about nine hundred years ago, and spent seven centuries undoing it (third-chair.md). It is mortmain's argument (people.md § The placeless), with the sums written on the board.

### Marriage across the line
- **What it costs the partner born to a place.** A marriage across the line brings no children into a place until the partner from outside is placed (Born to a place). A household that has a child outside gives up about an eighth of a householder. Someone born to a place who marries a placeless-born partner and is never drawn gives up two. By the reasoning of Why it holds, selection has made a first marriage across the line rarer than a birth outside, and felt the same way, as a wish and not a law.
- **Through the draw.** Where leave is drawn, a couple across the line enters the same draw as couples seeking a third, at the same odds: about one chance in 440 a year, 2% in ten years and 7% in thirty. A win places the partner, and the couple then has its two by right. The door by marriage is then no wider than the draw, by construction. Where leave is sold it is bought, and where blocks give it the block decides out loud.
- **Marrying late (proposal).** Most marriages across the line are second marriages, to a partner whose children already have households, when they cost a line nothing. Rome's widowers did the same: Marcus Aurelius took a concubine after Faustina died, so as not to put a stepmother over his children, and concubinage was an honourable union with a woman a man couldn't or wouldn't make his wife. Morganatic marriage did it for princes.
- **Widowhood.** The place goes down its own line at the holder's death, and a widow or widower from across the line has no claim on it: a year's grace in most drums, and then lodgings, the house or kin. Where the heir is a child, the trust that holds the place must let its rooms at the going rate.
- **The room it makes.** A couple across the line that is never placed is a household without an heir, and makes room like any other.

### Service
- **Rome's twenty-five years were paid for in lives.** At Roman mortality a good part of every intake died before its discharge. Hardly anyone dies on the back crews (the dose is capped at ~1 Sv a career; raft-transport.md), so a drum that placed everyone who served would have a door as wide as its volunteers.
- **So the crews elect.** A crew is a house (people.md), and it takes whom it chooses. With ~1,500 placings a year for service against ~45,000 a year who will be placed no other way, a berth on the long articles goes to about one in thirty. The election is the prize, and how a crew elects is watched as closely as the draw.
- **An illustrative split** of a drum's ~5,000 placings a year: ~3,300 by adoption into an heirless place (about one heirless household in fourteen names a living adult, often whoever cared for it), ~1,500 by service, ~200 by marriage. Adoptions come out of the households' half of the room and the other two out of the drum's, which leaves ~45,800 permits to draw: ~5,090 at each station ring, about seven hours' reading on Draw Day.

### The certificate
- **Standing is settled by birth.** A placeless-born person belongs to the register of the drum they were born in, and so does their claim on a commons. The Glide is free and an open drum's door is 140 seconds away, so without that rule every lenient drum would fill.
- **A drum writes a certificate** for one of its people who lodges elsewhere: it answers for their commons (~1.1 kW, paid to the host) and for any child born to them there, who is the home drum's placeless-born. England's parishes worked the same way from 1697, and what the certificate replaced was worse: a child born outside marriage was settled wherever it was born, so overseers moved unmarried pregnant women across the parish boundary before the birth.
- **A host's door is for its own.** An open drum's block places the children of its own households. A certificated lodger can work, marry and stay, and isn't in the block's room. A swap is different: it moves a person's standing with their share and place (people.md § Leaving), and the placeless-born have nothing to swap.

### Keepers from outside
- **The heirless have nieces and nephews.** people.md gives the placeless a drum's offices of trust, since nobody without an heir can pass an office on. But a registrar born outside has a brother or sister whose household is in the draw, and a clerk can mend a line in a register. The word nepotism was coined for the nephews of celibate popes.
- **So the draw's keepers come from somewhere else.** Tansy's are seven people from other drums of the Row, sworn for seven years. Italian communes hired their chief magistrate, the podestà, from another city for a year at a time, for the same reason.

## How leave is given
Room is one account. Each way of giving it out is a kind of drum, and most drums mix two.

### The draw
- **The rule.** Each year the drum issues as many permits for a third child as it has room it hasn't given another way, and draws them by lot among couples with two children and none outside. A drawn third pays nothing. In a holdings drum about half the room goes by naming and half by the draw; in a pool drum the draw is the lottery of unspent birth rights (people.md).
- **The odds.** Over all its years in the draw a couple has about one chance in eight where all the room is drawn, and one in sixteen where the heirless name half of it away. Most never win.
- **What makes it hold.** It is public and fair. The draw runs at every station ring, each permit is named for a household that ended without an heir, and winners often name the child for it, as children elsewhere are named for the dead whose share they carry. Nobody is exempt. A drum with a draw lives or dies by whether people believe the lot.
- **What it selects.** Nothing but entering, and everyone enters. It is the one way of giving leave under which nobody's descendants gain on anybody else's, and the one under which every household, rich or poor, has something to lose by a child outside. That is why most old drums draw.

### Gift
- **The rule.** The heirless give their room to whom they like: in a holdings drum by naming an heir, in a pool drum like Sorrel by giving a birth right to kin or friends (ashgrove.md). Only what nobody gave goes to the draw.
- **What it does.** A third is a relative's or a friend's present, and an heirless great-aunt is the most courted person in a family. It favours the well connected and those who ask hardest, and it puts pressure on the childless (economy.md § Children). Over deep time it selects for tending one's childless kin, which is not the worst thing a rule could breed.

### Sale
- **The rule.** Leave is sold: the drum's half by auction or at a fixed charge, or, in a pool drum like Lovage, birth rights from anyone who will part with theirs (economy.md).
- **What it does.** With room fixed and every couple wanting a third, the price is whatever the richest eighth of couples will pay, and it only rises as they grow richer. Thirds are a rich family's, and "third-born" carries a whiff of money. A household that can't pay has no odds to forfeit, so the children born outside are the poor's, and the placeless-born in a drum that sells are a class.

### The block's leave
- **The rule.** In open drums the drum's half of the room is the block's to give. A block of ~3,000 has about one third a year, and its court grants it out loud to a couple who asked (open-drums.md § Children without counting).
- **What it does.** Judgment stands where other drums have a rule. There is no forfeit and no keep; a block that loves a family finds it a third, or places the one it had. It works while neighbours know each other well enough to say no, and it selects for standing well with one's neighbours.

### The drum's own children (proposal; rare)
- **The rule.** The childless and the early dead aren't replaced by anyone's third. The clinic draws embryos by lot from every family's banked proofreading samples, and volunteers carry them; the children are raised by their carriers or by tables, and carry the drum's name.
- **What it does.** In a young Shade it was the one rule under which the wish didn't climb, since every family was equally likely to be drawn (The floor under the wish). Now it only takes the gift out of every family's hands. Few drums can stomach it.

## Where drums draw the line
### Rented rooms (Jeff)
Every holdings drum has to say what a child is when it is born to a couple in rented rooms, with no place of their own. Drums answer differently.

| Rule | What it looks like | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Banned | The child is placeless-born, with no later place, and the parents lose Hollow hours, deck seniority or posts (rung 2 of people.md's ladder); severity varies | The hardest line: few born outside, and nothing forgiven |
| Frowned on | The child is placeless-born, grows up crowded and long shareless, and takes its place if its parents' place comes or the line rule reaches them | The common line: a leak that a later place partly mends |
| Allowed | Rooms count as places: a child born in one is born to a place, and any couple who can rent raises children | No line at all: a third born without leave can rent, marry and raise children of its own, so a third costs its household nothing and nearly every household has one. Households multiply until every room is let, and the drum runs well over par |

- **What a room drum is like.** Young, crowded, cheerful and loud. Rents run two to four times a steady drum's (economy.md), which works as a price on everyone young, not only on would-be parents; the old who let rooms are rich and the renting young are a class; the commons thins per head; and the drum hits its first Dark harder than its neighbours. It also draws people who want big families from along the row, who swap in, which speeds it up and eases its neighbours.
- **Rent is the only brake it has.** With every couple wanting children and rooms a fixed stock, a room's rent climbs until couples wait for one as they would wait for a place. Where that leaves the drum depends on how many rooms its households will let.

### The keep (the posted price)
- **The rule (proposal; most steady drums).** A household is charged for the keep of a child born outside its allowance, from the Mill-table credit alone to the child's whole 1.1 kW of commons (economy.md § Children), until the child is grown or placed. The price is posted at every station ring.
- **What it does and doesn't.** It doesn't hold the drum's numbers; the line does that. It keeps the cost of a household's choice off its neighbours, as an English parish bound a child's father to indemnify it for the child's keep. A third born by leave pays nothing. Open drums post no price.

### The forfeit and the door
- **The forfeit.** In nearly every drum that draws, a household with a child outside is out of the draw. Strict drums add penalties: Hollow hours, deck seniority, posts.
- **The door** runs from shut (adoption only, and seldom) to wide (open drums, where blocks place most of their own). About one in ten placed is where having a child outside neither pays nor costs (Why it holds).

## Loosening and tightening
- **Every assembly has a party for loosening.** Most households want a third, and a vote can give it to them: count rooms as places, give every third a later place, widen the door, raise the allowance "this once" after a plague. The cost is spread over the whole drum and lands a generation later.
- **What loosening does.** With the wish saturated there is no slow climb. A drum whose line goes has three children a couple at once, and more where nothing stops at three: at three, 10% over par in ~25 years and the lean 1 kWe life in ~95 if rooms count as places, levelling off ~70% over par if they don't (people.md § Counted drums).
- **Tightening is slow, or hard.** A drum over par by its placeless-born can wait: most of them have no children, so the bulge is gone in a lifetime. A drum that can't wait has to cut births below deaths, and that means an allowance of fewer than two.
- **Standing can ration a third, not a first.** People wait decades for a third and take the lot's answer. Told they may not have a first or a second, they have it, and the drum has made most of a generation placeless-born at a stroke, which is no line at all. Württemberg and Bavaria found this in the nineteenth century: laws against the marriages of the poor moved the births outside marriage and stopped few of them (Knodel 1967). So a drum that must shrink licenses births by lot and fits its people: the Count (people.md).
- **The cycle.** A steady drum loosens and runs thin; a blight or a Dark finds it; it counts for a decade or two; it comes out steady again, sometimes with a Margin and open; a few generations on, nobody remembers the Count and the party for loosening wins a vote. Canon's rate is one serious overshoot or shock a drum about every 1,000 years (people.md). England ran one turn of it between the child allowances of the 1790s and the workhouse of 1834.
- **What stays put.** Old drums differ in how leave is given, where they draw the line, how wide the door is and how much room their clinics leave. None of them differs in whether its people want a third.

## Earth anchors
- **Heirs, not births.** Where land was short, Earth limited who could set up a household and who could inherit, never who could be born (people.md § Places and shares). A child born outside marriage was in English law *filius nullius*, nobody's heir, and still a child of its parish, which fed it and bound it apprentice.
- **The keep.** From 1576 English justices could order the parents of a child that fell on the parish to pay for it, and fathers gave bonds to indemnify the parish; from 1610 the mother could be sent to the house of correction for a year. It stayed the law until 1834.
- **A later marriage.** Canon law made a child legitimate when its parents married. At Merton in 1236 England's barons refused to follow it, and English law held out until 1926. Drums split the same way over a later place.
- **Both parents.** From 451/0 BC an Athenian citizen needed two citizen parents, and the *Constitution of the Athenians* gives the reason as the number of citizens. Pericles, who proposed the law, later had to ask the people to enrol his own son by a foreign woman.
- **Service.** Rome's auxiliaries served twenty-five years for citizenship and the right of lawful marriage, which made their children citizens.
- **A line that passes down.** German guilds asked proof of legitimate birth of an apprentice, of a master and of a master's wife. Laslett's "bastardy-prone sub-society" is the argument that illegitimacy ran in a few interrelated families; later work doubts they were a people apart.
- **Moving the births.** Nineteenth-century German laws against the marriages of the poor raised illegitimacy and barely lowered births, and the ratios fell when the laws went (Knodel 1967).
- **Loosening.** Many parishes in southern England paid allowances by the child from the 1790s, births rose where they did (Boyer 1989), and the New Poor Law of 1834 answered with the workhouse.
- **A living for life.** English dower gave a widow a life interest in a third of her husband's land, reverting to his heir. A corrody was a monastery's promise of food, clothing and a room for life. Guilds and friendly societies kept a box their members paid into and drew on. And medieval manor courts recorded agreements by which the old handed a holding to someone, kin or stranger, who would keep them (Clark 1982): the adopted carer.
- **Unions that carried no inheritance.** Roman concubinage and the morganatic marriages of German princes were recognised unions whose partner and children took no rank and no estate (Treggiari 1981).
- **Settlement.** From 1662 an English parish owed relief only to its own settled poor, and from 1697 a person could live in another parish on a certificate by which the home parish promised to take them back. A child born outside marriage was settled where it was born, so parishes harried unmarried pregnant women over their boundaries before the birth.
- **A magistrate from elsewhere.** The podestà of an Italian commune was usually chosen from another city, for a year or less, so that he owed nothing to any local family.
- **Standing by number.** Earth's lines were drawn at marriage. The Shade's is drawn at a number, and the nearest Earth case is recent and coercive: China's children born outside the plan were widely refused household registration, and they were about three in five of the ~13 million people the 2010 census found unregistered, until the State Council ordered everyone registered in 2016. The Shade's line takes less from the child, which is registered, fed and schooled, and asks its parents for nothing but the keep. Whether a line drawn at a number can be as old and as calm as one drawn at marriage is this doc's largest assumption.

## How a household passes
Every kind of household below needs an allowance now. The lines on the wish say what each rule did to the climb while there was one (the 6 Nov models); what each rule still decides is who carries a third, and what families are like.

### Whole to one heir (canon)
- **The rule.** A place passes whole to one heir, usually a great-grandchild in their late 20s; the other children marry heirs elsewhere, wait, or stay placeless (people.md). Heirs marry by age and liking, not by a partner's birth order or share.
- **Who carries an extra child.** Where no line is drawn at the birth, everyone a little: a third's chance of a household is every child's chance, so its cost spreads over the whole drum as a slightly longer wait for everyone, a few more placeless and a thinner commons. A share-based marriage market changes nothing, since a third's share deficit lands on some child's wait, not on anyone's marriage. Primogeniture, the heir fixed as the eldest, takes a little off a third's advantage, since the extra child is always a younger one. So canon's kind of household needs the line drawn at the birth: a third born by leave has a place waiting, and a third born without it has none (Born to a place).
- **The wish** climbed at nearly the full speed it would have with a place for every adult; stem places cap a drum's numbers, not the selection.

### Portions (proposal)
- **The rule.** The place still passes whole to one heir. The other children each carry a portion when they marry: a share of the family's savings, belongings and Hollow hours, and, where fixed goods are owned and inherited (economy.md's gentry drums), a claim on rents. An heir marries the best portion they can, and the incoming portion pays out the heir's own siblings, as the Irish farmer's bride's fortune paid his sisters' (Arensberg and Kimball 1940). Three children split what two would have.
- **Family life.** Matches and matchmakers; the portion a public number; marriage late, after the bargaining; a respectable class of unmarried aunts and uncles; the younger children of big families marrying placeless partners or entering houses. "A third is a mortgage on its sisters' matches."
- **The wish.** In the family tree, heirs who chose by portion took a third's advantage from ~0.94 to ~0.85 when three couples in ten wanted a third, and from ~0.82 to ~0.65 when half did. Most households come down the line rule at a death, not through a marriage, so the market reaches only part of who heads one. Where heirs were named young and married strictly by portion, the advantage fell to the floor and the first Count came about two-fifths later than by custom. Portions never held the wish; they decide who pays for a third: the younger children of big families. Today a third born by leave carries a portion like its brothers and sisters, and a child born outside carries none.
- **How it fails.** Portions make wealth tell in every marriage, and a gentry of portions marries itself.

### Split estates (proposal)
- **The rule.** No heir. The place and the family's shares are one estate, split equally among the children as each marries, with a room kept for the old as Central European farmers kept the *Altenteil*. A couple may have children only if their holdings together reach a minimum, as Württemberg and Bavaria made the poor prove their means before marrying in the nineteenth century. The minimum is this kind's line: a child born to a couple under it is placeless-born.
- **Family life.** Small homes; marriages that assemble a holding from two thin ones; equality between brothers and sisters; an early start; no placeless and no heirs. Poverty when the minimum creeps down.
- **The wish.** It climbed anyway. A spouse's holding makes up for a third child's thin one, so the in-laws carry part of the cost, and a split-estate drum holds no fixed number of households for the floor to bind. Over the long run a third's advantage averages a little above the floor while few want one and two to three times it once most do. The numbers swing: a generation born when holdings are thick is followed by a thin one, from a third of par to 1.6× in a model of whole generations (overlapping lives would damp it, and so would couples free to re-match). Requiring each partner, not each couple, to meet the minimum makes both worse. With the wish saturated every holding is split down to the minimum, and the minimum is the whole of the drum's birth policy.
- **How it fails.** Without a law the minimum creeps down, as Ireland's plots did before the Famine. The German laws mostly moved births outside marriage instead of stopping them (Knodel 1967). Split-estate drums are rare and seldom last.

### Sibling households (proposal)
- **The rule.** A place stays with the sibling group for life. Partners visit and never move, as among the Mosuo (Cai Hua 2001) and in the Nayar's matrilineal houses of Kerala. Children belong to their mother's place, or to whichever place the couple names at the birth, and uncles raise their sisters' children. A place is sized for a lineage, a sibling group of two to four in each generation and ~7–14 people at once (three to six times canon's 78 m², and that many fewer places), and when a generation outgrows it the excess leave for houses and Margin flats.
- **Family life.** Big households three generations deep; no wedding ever moves anyone; few couples share a home; every adult is an aunt or an uncle to someone in the next room. Tibet's fraternal polyandry kept holdings whole the same way and left many women unmarried (Goldstein 1976).
- **The wish.** If the excess left by lot, it climbed as fast as by custom; if a mother's third and later children left first, at about two and a half to three times the floor once most couples wanted a third: slower than custom, never still. Today the place's size is the allowance, and where third-born leave first the leavers are known from birth.
- **How it fails.** Over who stays. A household that sends its own away when it overflows has a quarrel in every generation.

## The floor under the wish
Why the Shade is saturated, as the 6 Nov models gave it. The arithmetic is exact for a drum in which a couple who wants a third may have one. An allowance holds the numbers and leaves the argument standing for whoever is given leave.

- **Why there is a floor.** A drum at par replaces each couple with a couple. If one couple in twenty has no children, the couples who have children must leave 2 ÷ 0.95 ≈ 2.1 children who head households of their own, on average. A family of two can leave at most two, so the families with a third must make up the difference: they out-breed families of two by at least 0.105 ÷ their share. Counting the few who die before 35 the room is a little more, ~0.12, which is canon's ~12% of families with a third at par; the models leave the early dead out. It's an accounting identity for any drum whose households are fixed by its places and filled from its own children.
- **What selection sees.** Where some couples who want a third don't get one (a draw, a price), the families who have one still carry the identity, and the advantage of wanting a third is the same room shared among everyone who wants one. So the floor is set by the share who want a third, which is the share who have one only where nothing rations thirds. It assumes couples who don't want a third don't have one, and that childlessness has nothing to do with the wish. The childfree, who want none, are replaced like the infertile, and selection thins them too.
- **What a sibling's cost can and can't do.** A rule that makes a third cost its siblings (a smaller portion, a poorer match, a place that comes later) only moves households between children, since the drum has the same places either way. The room the childless leave still goes to someone's extra child, and only families with extras have one. Lack's principle tunes birds' clutch sizes because an extra chick can starve its brood and fledge none. An extra child in a drum starves no one; it grows up and takes a vacancy.
- **The floor falls as the wish spreads.** At par it is a whole child: every third finds a household. When three couples in ten want a third it is a third of a child, and when most do it is a tenth. Rules that rank children by family size or draw thirds by lot reach the floor by construction, since they place every child of a two-child family first. Rules that leave every child alike let the advantage run near a whole child.

  The models stop at three children. Where births aren't rationed, a wish that has reached everyone would go on to fourths, so the last row is a point on the way, not an end.
- **How a household passes sets where the advantage sits above the floor.** From the family tree (canon's line rule, 120-year lives) and the generation model (whole generations, b = 0.5, four runs):

  Canon's line-rule row is the family tree at 30% and 50% (by age) and every child's chance beyond. Split estates hold no fixed number of households, so the identity doesn't bind them: their advantage swings from one generation to the next, sometimes below zero, and the figures are long averages with errors of up to ±0.1.
- **Earth runs the same arithmetic.** Where completed family size passes from parents to children, by genes or by upbringing, the lines with more children grow, and fertility rises again after a transition unless new low-fertility ways of living keep arriving faster than selection can catch them (Kolk, Cownden and Enquist 2014; Collins and Page 2019). The drum's models take the parent–child regression of the wish at 0.2–0.5. A Shade with a fast churn of fashions would sit at the low end or below, and everything here would run slower.
- **How fast it went.** From a fresh spread of wishes (`models/ratchet.py`): where custom alone held births, a drum at par reached 10% over and its first Count in ~450–1,000 years, ~300–450 where rented rooms raised children and ~600–1,400 where portions or birth order decided every household. Under a price or a market in rights the numbers held and the strength of the wish climbed a standard deviation every ~800–2,000 years, the price with it. Under a draw half of couples wanted a third after ~1,500–3,800 years and four in five or more after ~10,000. Medicine that left fewer couples childless lowered the floor, to a fifth at one in a hundred, and slowed the last of these. Every road ended in the same place.

## What it feels like
- **Wanting a third is the Shade's commonest ache.** Nearly everyone grew up wanting more children than they would have, and so did their parents. Tables of aunts, fostering and houses are how a drum meets a want it can't grant.
- **Draw Day.** The year's permits are drawn at every station ring, each read out with the name of the household that ended without an heir. Winners are congratulated like newlyweds. Couples who enter for twenty-five years and never win are a recognised kind of person, and so are the ones who stop entering.
- **The third chair.** A household with a child born outside raised it like the other two and loved it the same. The child knows from the register what it isn't, its brother and sister know what they are, and the parents know they bet on the door. Most families never speak of it, and every family has an opinion about the families that did it.
- **Coming of age.** On the day a brother's dividend goes into his own name, nothing arrives for the child born outside, and the keep comes off the household's page.
- **Marrying late.** A widow, or a man whose children have their places, marries someone born outside, and nobody enters a draw for it. It is the commonest way a placeless-born person comes to live in a place, and it ends at a reading.
- **Named for nobody.** Children are named for the dead whose shares they carry, and permits for households that ended. Someone born outside who is never placed leaves neither.
- **Named for a place.** A third born by leave is the heir of a household that ended, carries its name, and grows up visiting the old couple whose home it will have.
- **The courted aunt.** In drums where room goes by gift, the heirless are flattered, visited and fed for forty years, and know why.
- **The price as a class line.** Where leave is sold, thirds cluster among the comfortable, and the placeless-born are the poor's children. People there can tell which third a child is by its family's deck.
- **The match.** In portion drums families bargain, portions are public, and a big family's youngest marries late or enters a house. Gossip is about portions the way other drums gossip about places.
- **Uncles.** In sibling households a child's mother's brothers raise it and its father visits. Inheritance never moves anyone; leaving does, and the leavers are honoured at a farewell meal.
- **Rooms.** In room drums the landlords are old and the tenants are young families; rent is the whole of politics, and the neighbours grumble about the swaps.

## Open questions
- Which kinds of drum sit where on Merrow, and their names.
- How many children are born outside the allowance and how many of them are placed: canon takes ~3% of births and one in ten.
- How old the Shade must be for the leak to be that small. If the habit of keeping to a line was ever lost, it takes ~8,000–19,000 years of shut doors, or ~45,000–110,000 at one in ten placed, to bring births outside back to one household in twenty (people.md leaves the Shade's age open).
- What keeps one couple in twenty childless in a Shade where selection has thinned everyone who wanted no children. If it is nearer one in fifty, one family in twenty is given a third and the door has to be that much narrower.
- Whether steady drums fit their placeless-born, and how many do. It ends the line outside, and it is counting one group.
- How a marriage across the line is treated where leave is sold or given by gift. Where it is drawn the couple enters the draw, and in open drums the block decides (What the placeless-born live on § Marriage across the line).
- Whether houses hold shares as well as places. A house with shares could pay its members a dividend, and would be mortmain twice over.
- What a drum's rule owes a widow or widower from across the line, beyond a year's grace, when the place is held in trust for a child.
- Whether a drum may refuse to write a certificate, and what a host drum asks for a lodger's room.
- How the crews elect, and whether a crew that places its own becomes a line that keeps to itself.
- How strongly habits pass from parents to children over thousands of years. The models take a regression of 0.2–0.5; a fast churn of fashions would lower it (Kolk et al. 2014), and a line outside would grow more slowly.
- The keep in kWh, and what a household owes that can't pay it.
- Whether any drum raises the drum's own children, and who raises them.
- **How canon squares Sorrel's ~23,000-year Register with the floor:** answered in Where the Shade is: the wish is saturated in every drum, and most people want a third.
- **How drums with a draw answer thirds born without a permit:** answered in Born to a place: the child is placeless-born, and its household is out of the draw.

## Method
- **The placing model** (`models/placing.py`, output in `models/out/placing.txt`), for a drum with the wish saturated. Room is 1 − (1 − c) × s, with c the share of couples childless (5%) and s survival to 35 (0.993): 5.7% of the births that would refill a drum's households, and a third for 5.7% × 2.01 ÷ 0.95 ≈ 12% of fertile families. *The ledger:* with a leak L (births outside the allowance, per replacement birth) and a door d (the share of them placed), placings are dL, thirds by leave are room − dL, the drum sits (1 − d)L over par and the placeless-born are (1 − d)L ÷ (1 + (1 − d)L) of its people. *Whether a child outside pays:* a household that keeps to its allowance leaves 2 + (its chance of leave) householders, and one with a child outside leaves 2 + d and is out of the draw. Having a child outside is a threshold trait (2% of households by habit and 1% by accident at the start) passed on with regression b = 0.2–0.5, and its spread follows the breeder's equation; a waiting household's chance of leave is the thirds left divided by the households still waiting, so waiting pays more the commoner the habit is, and the same run started at four households in five gives the times for a large leak to mend. *The line outside:* each generation's placeless-born are the newly born outside (trait mean zero) plus the children of the last generation's; one couple in 25 has children at the start, three each; only those couples are the next generation's parents, so their children's mean moves by b × the selection differential. The line is followed mixed in, half apart, wholly apart, and with old lines marrying only old lines, which removes all dilution and bounds how fast a line can take off.
- **A placeless-born life** (`models/placeless_life.py`, output in `models/out/placeless_life.txt`): sums, not a simulation, on canon's figures. *The keep:* k × 8,766 h × 18 years, and a household's wallets (4 × 0.9 − k) ÷ 5 kW a head. *Income:* 3.1 kWh × 2,000 h ÷ 365 ≈ 17 kWh a day. *Old age:* to live on 10 kWh a day from the age work stops to 120 at no interest, bank 10 × (years after) ÷ (years worked); at 3%, the annuity's cost at 85 over the value of a constant payment from 18. The box: x × 17 kWh × 54 years ÷ 35 years. *Waiting shares:* at par shares equal people, so the ~3% of people shareless in `stem_places.py` are matched by as many shares "held for the next child born there": ~6 M × 0.9 kW ≈ 5.4 GW; the commons is (400 GW − the wallets paid) ÷ 205.4 M people. Age shares are from `stem_places.py`'s mortality: 85% of a steady population is over 18, 77% over 28, 30% over 85 and 18% over 100. *Houses:* the placeless-born past 28 × 35 m² ÷ 78 m² a place; people = households × 2.22 × 1.027. *The draw:* a household's lifetime chance of leave (11%) halved where the heirless name half the room, over 25 years of entering: ~0.23% a year. *The doors:* an assumed split of 3,300, 1,500 and 200 placings a year.
- **The 6 Nov models below** start from a fresh spread of wishes. They give how the Shade came to be saturated, not what a drum looks like now.
- **The floor.** At par the households that have children must average 2 ÷ (1 − c) children who head households, where c is the share childless (~5%). A family of two leaves at most 2, so families with a third average at least 2 + (2 ÷ (1 − c) − 2) ÷ p, with p their share; where thirds are rationed, the same room spread over the share who want one bounds the advantage of wanting one. It holds exactly for a drum whose households are fixed by its places and filled from its own children, where couples who don't want a third never have one and childlessness is independent of the wish. Children who die young or only children raise the floor (to ~0.12 ÷ p with the early dead); households of one would lower it; split estates, which fix no number of households, are outside it. Rules that place every child of a two-child family before any other child reach it exactly.
- **The generation model** (`models/regimes.py`): ~4,000 places, whole generations of ~35 years, a heritable wish for a third (child = mean + b × (midparent − mean) + noise, variance held at 1), a couple having a third when the mean of their wishes passes a threshold set for 10.5% at the start, 5% of couples childless by chance, never more than three children. One regime per inheritance or birth rule; the advantage selection sees is the difference in children who head households between couples who wanted a third and couples who didn't, credited to the generation of those couples. `python3 regimes.py table` prints the advantage by the share wanting a third, mean ± standard error over four runs at b = 0.5 (the table above), and `python3 regimes.py all` the trajectories. Rank, order, the draw and rights to the most eager reach the floor by construction, so the model confirms the arithmetic for them; the family tree is the realistic test of portions. The model's stem places pass to the eldest child. Split-estate drums start with holdings of 0.5 ± 30% of a place each, a minimum of 0.7 of a place a couple, and couples paired once with no re-matching. Sibling households hold 4 or 12 adults a generation; each woman's partner is drawn from the men kept in other places, and every man kept counts as heading a household.
- **The family tree** (`models/share_market.py`, on `models/stem_places.py`): ~15,000 people with 120-year lives and canon's line rule, the wish raised to 2.18 or 2.38 children a couple, every household whose brood is born in a 30-year window followed for 120 years. Four marriage markets: by age (canon), heirs taking only share-holders, like marrying like by share, and heirs choosing the largest portion (1 ÷ the number of siblings).
- **The cohort model** (`models/ratchet.py`): an age-structured drum of 200 M at 120-year lives, with the wish's mean rising by b × the selection differential each cohort; b = 0.2, 0.35 and 0.5; stem and ranked drums settle 1.5× their excess births over par; a Count at 10% over clears to 2% under over ~15 years by lot and pauses selection. Regimes: custom (every child alike), rent pushing back, ranked (the floor, places capping births), rooms (a home for every couple), the draw (the floor, births held at par), a price or market (the most eager tenth pay), the drum's own children (no selection). Its medicine section reruns custom and the draw with 2% and 1% childless, from a wish set for the new par and from one set for canon's 5%.
- **First Counts** quoted are from par with b = 0.5 (fastest) to 0.2 (slowest); the draw's saturation times likewise.
