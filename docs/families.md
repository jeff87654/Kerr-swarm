## Summary
- **The Shade wants more children than it can have.** About one couple in twenty has no children, so the families with a third replace them, and wanting a third pays by at least the room they leave (The floor under the wish). Nothing a drum can live with removes that, so within a few thousand years of any start most couples want a third. The Shade is far older than that and every new drum is founded by an old one's people, so there is no drum where it hasn't happened.
- **So drums don't hold the wish. They hold who is born to a place.** A household's allowance is two children by right and a third by leave. Leave comes out of the room the childless and the early dead leave, about one birth in eighteen: enough for one family in eight.
- **Most drums hold it with the fitting (Jeff, 10 Nov).** Everyone is fitted with a contraceptive implant at fourteen. It comes out at the clinic by right, for two children, once a couple has a place, and again for a third on a permit. So a child is conceived only where a place is waiting, and nearly every child is born to one. The clinic sets one embryo a pregnancy, so twins can't be bred for (Twins).
- **Standing is the law behind the implant.** A child born outside the allowance, after a couple has cut its implants out or a clinic has made a mistake, is placeless-born. It is registered, fed and schooled like any child, and grows up in its parents' home. What it lacks is a claim: it can't inherit a place, be named heir to one or bring children into one, unless it is placed. Its household is out of the draw. In a fitted drum that is about one birth in 400 and one person in 430.
- **Why standing alone isn't enough.** Two things undo it over the Shade's age (`models/placeless_share.py`). A door of fixed size, so many adoptions or crew berths a year, fills from behind: households go on having children outside until the door's odds fall to what a household forfeits, so births outside settle at seven to fifteen for every placing, and a wider door makes more placeless-born. And the placeless-born have nothing to forfeit. The willingness to have children with no place is selected among them and carried back into the households by everyone who is placed, so their line takes off within ~70,000–220,000 years. Drums that hold by standing alone cycle to Counts, a Count puts an implant in every arm, and most drums kept it when the Count ended.
- **The door is the lot, set as odds.** About one in seventeen of those born outside is placed, by the same draw that gives thirds. That is half a household's chance of leave, so a child outside costs its household more than it gains, however many or few are born.
- **Their poverty does no work.** The line is drawn in householders, not kWh, so how well a drum keeps its placeless-born moves neither its numbers nor the wish. A fitted drum has few enough to keep them like anyone: from eighteen each holds a waiting wallet, the dividend of a share that is waiting for a child (What the placeless-born live on).
- **The default's cost is that a stop is one vote away.** A fitted drum and a counted drum have the same implant in the same arms. What separates them is that two are by right: the clinic takes the implants out for any couple with a place on the Register's word alone. The clinicians' guild holds that rule across the Shade, the keepers of a draw come from other drums, and the right of the Glide stands.
- **A few drums choose otherwise,** about four in a hundred (Drums that chose otherwise). Free drums fit nobody and hold by standing: about one person in forty is placeless-born, shareless and at the door of a house, and a line outside is growing. Marked drums fit only those born outside. Service drums let the shareless have the children they can pay for, and a fifth to a third of their people are servants at the survival floor. With no rule at all a drum is at twice par, the most its hull can cool, in one to three centuries.
- **Drums differ in how leave is given and in what takes the implant out.** Leave goes by lot (the draw), by gift from the heirless, by sale, or by a block's say. The implants come out for a place, at a wedding, or, in room drums, for a lease.
- **Counting is licences below two.** A drum that has to shrink can't do it by standing: people will wait for a third and won't wait for a first. So it leaves the implants in and draws lots for first and second children: the Count (people.md § Counted drums).
- **How a household passes still differs:** whole to one heir (canon's stem places), to one heir with portions for the rest, split among all the children, or kept by the siblings together for life. It decides who carries a third and what families are like.

## Where the Shade is
- **The clock ran out long ago.** From a fresh spread of wishes, half of couples want a third after ~1,500–3,800 years under a draw and four in five or more after ~10,000; where custom alone held births it went faster (The floor under the wish). The Shade has stood closed, at fixed numbers, for far longer than that: Sorrel's Register alone runs back ~23,000 years, and the growth horizon is 1–2 Gyr.
- **No drum starts fresh.** New drums are founded at the rafts' edges by old drums' placeless (economy.md § The frontier). A raft's drums grow by ~1.4–3.6 × 10⁻⁹ a year, so only one to four drums in 100,000 are under 10,000 years old, and their founders brought the old wish with them.
- **What saturated means.** Most couples in every drum want a third, and would have a fourth. The wish is no longer something a rule can turn up or down. A drum can only decide which children have a place, and what selection still works on is narrower: whatever gets a child one.
- **How old it has to be.** Saturation takes a few thousand years. In a drum that fits nobody, births outside take ~7,000–30,000 years to fill a door, and a line outside takes some tens to hundreds of thousands of years to take off (Drums that chose otherwise). people.md leaves the Shade's age at story time open; this doc assumes it is well past the first, as Sorrel's Register suggests, and old enough for the last to have happened to most drums at least once.
- **What the climbs are now.** The first Counts and climbing wishes of the 6 Nov models describe the Shade's first ten thousand years. They are not a cross-section of the Shade today. What a drum faces now if it takes its line away is faster: three children a couple at once, and more where nothing stops at three (Loosening and tightening).

## Born to a place
### The allowance
- **Two by right, a third by leave (proposal).** Every household with a place may have two children. A third needs leave, and leave is issued only as fast as there is room.
- **Room.** One couple in twenty has no children and a few people die before 35, so two children a household leave about one slot in eighteen unfilled (5.7% of a drum's births). That is the drum's room: ~95,000 children a year in a nation drum, a third child for one family in eight.
- **Whose room it is.** In a holdings drum room has an address. A household that ends without a child leaves a place; the place needs an heir, and the heir a spouse, so it makes room for two. The household names its heir, usually a child not yet born, three generations down a brother's or sister's line, and the naming is that child's leave. The second child, who will marry in somewhere, is the drum's to give: by the draw, by sale or by the block (How leave is given). A child who dies young is replaced by right.
- **Born to a place.** A child born inside the allowance is born to a place. It can inherit one, be named heir to one or marry into one, and its own first two children will be born to a place in turn. The clinic records it at the birth and the drum's register carries it beside the name.
- **Both parents.** A household's allowance needs both partners born to a place, or placed. A marriage across the line is a marriage, but it brings no children into the place until the partner from outside is placed. Athens asked the same of its citizens (Earth anchors).
- **Medicine sets the room.** Room is whatever a drum's childless leave. Selection has long since thinned people who want no children, as it thins anything that leaves fewer descendants, so canon's one couple in twenty are mostly people who wanted children and didn't have them: never paired, parted or widowed early, a place that came too late, a house entered young, what the clinics can't mend. A drum with better clinics and better luck has less room and a narrower door:

| Couples childless | Room, of births | A third for one family in | A household's chance of leave | The door, at half of it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 in 12 | 8.6% | 5.3 | 19% | 1 in 11 |
| 1 in 20 (canon) | 5.7% | 8.3 | 12% | 1 in 17 |
| 1 in 33 | 3.7% | 13 | 7.6% | 1 in 26 |
| 1 in 50 | 2.7% | 18 | 5.5% | 1 in 36 |
| 1 in 100 | 1.7% | 29 | 3.4% | 1 in 58 |

  (For a fitted drum, with one birth in 400 outside the allowance. Identical twins at a second birth take 0.2% of births out of each row's room before any leave is given: 3.5% of it at canon and 12% in the last row. A drum whose clinics improve has less room at once, and for a generation more children than slots.)

### The fitting
- **The rule (Jeff's decision, 10 Nov 2026; the particulars are proposals).** In most drums everyone is fitted with a contraceptive implant at fourteen: a rod the length of a matchstick under the skin of the upper arm, good for decades. A clinic takes one out in a minute.
- **Out by right.** A couple with a place, both of them born to a place or placed, has its implants out for the asking, for two children. No board, lot or clerk decides it. The clinic checks the Register and takes them out. They go back at the second birth, and come out again on a permit for a third (How leave is given).
- **One embryo a pregnancy.** Every licensed child begins at the clinic, so the clinic decides how many embryos a pregnancy carries, and the rule is one (Twins).
- **What it does.** A child is conceived only where a place is waiting. A birth before a place, a third without leave and a child of the placeless-born each take a deliberate act by two people. Births outside the allowance fall from the few in a hundred of a drum that holds by standing alone to about one in 400 (The leak).
- **It makes a rule of what people already did.** People have their children once they have a household (people.md § Places and shares), and every child a couple means to have begins at a clinic, with proofreading. So nearly everyone in the Shade was using contraception from their teens into their thirties before any drum required it. The fitting makes the long-acting kind the default and the Register the key.
- **Standing is still the law behind it.** The implant is easy to cut out, and drums leave it so: a drum that fits everyone wants to be able to say that nobody is locked. What a household loses by a cut is what it always lost by a child outside. The child is placeless-born and the household is out of the draw (Why it holds).
- **Where it came from.** No drum voted in a quiet year to fit all its children. Every old drum has counted (people.md § Counted drums), and a Count puts an implant in every arm. When the stop ended the assembly gave back two by right, and nobody would move that the implants come out of the fourteen-year-olds. Britain's income tax, brought back in 1842 for three years, was renewed the same way. After a few turns of that, nearly every drum fits.
- **What drums differ on is what takes it out.** A place (most drums); a wedding between two people born to a place; or a lease (room drums). See Where drums draw the line.
- **What it costs:**
  - **It is done to every child's body by the drum.** Earth has no old precedent for it, only for the rule it enforces (Earth anchors). People who can't accept it glide to a free drum, and the few free drums are every fitted drum's conscience clause. A clause inside the drum wouldn't hold: nearly everyone wants a third, so nearly everyone would claim it. England's vaccination law got one in 1898, and once it was eased in 1907 a quarter of births were exempted.
  - **A stop is one vote away.** The whole machinery of a counted drum is already in every arm, and all that separates the two is that two are by right. So the default's safeguards are the ones that keep that true. The clinic takes the implants out for any couple with a place and fewer than two children on the Register's word alone, and clinicians hold that as their guild's rule across the Shade, whatever a drum's assembly has voted (people.md § Counted drums). The keepers of the draw come from other drums. The right of the Glide stands.
  - **So is a third for everyone.** The implant doesn't quiet the party for loosening (Loosening and tightening). It only makes the result of its vote exact.

### The placeless-born
- **What they have.** A register entry, the commons, school, their parents' home while they grow, the fitting at fourteen like everyone, the right of the Glide and the right to marry. In a fitted drum they hold a wallet from eighteen (What the placeless-born live on).
- **What they lack.** A claim on any place. The line rule passes over them, their implant stays in unless they are placed, and a child born to them is placeless-born too.
- **Who they are.** In a fitted drum, about one birth in 400. Most are the children of couples who spent years in the draw and then cut their implants out, or glided to a free drum's clinic and came home. Some are the clinics' mistakes: an implant not put back after a second birth, one left in past its date, a dead batch. A mistake changes nothing in the register, since an allowance that forgave mistakes would find them made on purpose. A few are the children of the placeless-born.
- **They aren't the wild-born.** A child conceived after a cut begins outside the clinic, so it isn't proofread: it carries its own seventy or so new mutations, as everyone on Earth did. But it is born at the clinic or brought to it and registered, and the clinic records what it would have put back and puts it back in the child's own children, if there are any. A wild-born child (people.md) is one the clinics never see.
- **Their lives.** A wallet, a home in a parent's place, a rented room or a house, a calling, a marriage if they like, and no nursery. In a fitted drum they are few: seven in a block of 3,000, two in a school of 800.

### Placing
- **The door is the lot (proposal).** A placeless-born person who is placed counts from then on as born to a place, and the implant comes out for two children once they hold a place or marry into one. In a fitted drum there is one way in. Every unplaced adult born outside is entered in the draw each year from eighteen to sixty, and the drum sets the number drawn so that about one in seventeen is placed in a lifetime: one chance in ~680 a year, ~250 placings a year in a nation drum, 28 read out at each station ring.
- **Why odds and not a number.** A door of fixed size has odds that depend on how many are born outside. Where households can have children outside at will, they go on doing it until those odds fall to what a household forfeits. So births outside settle at about seven for every placing where the forfeit is all of a household's leave, and fifteen where it is the draw alone, and a drum that widens such a door gets more placeless-born, not fewer (Drums that chose otherwise § Free drums). A door fixed as odds below the forfeit pays nobody, however many or few are born outside.
- **Why no other doors.** Adoption by the heirless, long service and a marriage the drum allows are all doors a household can aim a child at: a great-aunt who will name it, a crew that elects its own. The lot can't be aimed. An heirless household still names its heir, a child unborn or anyone born to a place. Naming someone born outside is a placing, and placings go by the lot.
- **A placing is a permit with a place behind it.** Like a third's permit it is read in the name of a household that ended. Someone placed who has no place to marry into is given that household's, out of the drum's pool of heirless places.
- **Every placing spends room.** A placed person fills a slot a third would have filled, so the door and the draw come out of one account. At 250 a year against ~91,000 thirds by leave it is a quarter of a percent of the room.
- **A later place.** In a drum where the implants come out at the wedding, a child may be born before its parents' place arrives. It takes its place when the place comes, as canon law made a child legitimate by its parents' later marriage, and it costs no room, since the child is one of the household's two. Strict drums refuse, as English law did for seven centuries (Earth anchors).
- **Founding is no door.** New drums are founded by the placeless (economy.md § The frontier), but a standing drum sees one founder's share about every three and a half years, against ~4,200 children born outside its allowance each year.

### Why it holds
- **The implant holds the numbers, and standing holds the implant.** A cut takes two people and a blade. What keeps households from it is the sum that always kept them from a child outside.
- **What a household weighs,** counted in children who will head households. A household that keeps to its allowance leaves two, plus its chance of a third by leave, about 0.12. One that has a child outside leaves two, plus the chance that child is placed, and it is out of the draw for good. So the door decides it:

| Of the placeless-born, placed | A household gains by having a child outside | What the habit of doing so does |
|---|---|---|
| None | −0.12 of a child | Halves in ~9,000 years or more, down to accidents |
| 1 in 17 (the fitted default) | −0.06 | Halves in ~12,000 years, down to the clinics' mistakes and the few who don't care |
| 1 in 10 | −0.02 | Holds level |
| 1 in 4 | +0.13 | Doubles in ~6,000 years or more |
| Half | +0.39 | Doubles in ~2,000–5,000 years |
| All | +0.91 | Doubles in ~900–2,200 years; placings take all the room in ~2,000 |

- **The door can be no wider than the draw, and is safer well inside it.** A drum can place about as large a share of its placeless-born as the share of its households it gives a third, one in eight, and no more. Past that, having a child outside and hoping is a better bet than waiting. The default sets the door at half the draw.
- **The forfeit matters as much.** If a household with a child outside keeps its place in the draw, having one costs nothing even with the door shut. And the draw's room can't go to placing a child of the household that wins it, or every household would have its third first and enter after.
- **Why a price never did this.** A charge in kWh is paid by whoever can afford it, so a priced third is a comfortable family's, and the poor family's third is born anyway. A child's standing costs every family the same, and it is paid in the one thing selection counts: grandchildren with households. Where leave is sold, a household that can't pay has no odds to lose, and for it any door pays.
- **What selection has made of people (proposal).** Selection counts children who head households (people.md § What births do to the gene pool). In drums where a child outside the allowance is a line that ends, it has favoured, over the Shade's age, people who want children they can place, and who would no more have one outside than their ancestors would have had one with no farm to leave it. People ache for a third and mostly don't want one born outside. The fitting changes none of that. It takes away the accidents and the births before a place, and leaves the ache where it was.

### The leak
- **About one birth in 400 in a fitted drum (proposal).** With both partners fitted, a failure of the implants themselves is vanishingly rare. What is left is the cut, the glide to a free drum's clinic, and the clinic's own mistakes. Earth's implants show the last: in Australia's first three years with one, about one insertion in a thousand ended in a pregnancy, and the commonest cause was an implant that had never gone in.
- **Without the fitting it is a few in a hundred.** England's parish registers show 1–7% of births outside marriage from the 1500s to the 1950s, under a rule of the same shape and without contraception. A drum that holds by standing alone sits there (Drums that chose otherwise).
- **What a fitted drum carries.** The placeless-born who aren't placed are extra lifetimes over par:

| Births outside the allowance | A year | Placed a year | Carried | Of the drum's people | Over par |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 in 1,000 | 1,700 | 100 | 0.19 M | 1 in 1,070 | 0.09% |
| 1 in 400 (canon) | 4,200 | 250 | 0.47 M | 1 in 430 | 0.23% |
| 1 in 200 | 8,300 | 500 | 0.94 M | 1 in 210 | 0.47% |
| 1 in 100 | 16,700 | 1,000 | 1.9 M | 1 in 110 | 0.94% |

  (One in seventeen placed. A third goes to one family in 8.3 in every row, by leave or as a twin.)
- **So a fitted drum sits a quarter of a percent over par.** A nation drum of 200 M shares holds ~200.5 M people, ~470,000 of them placeless-born. Each year ~4,200 children are born outside the allowance and ~250 people are placed; ~91,000 thirds are born by leave and ~3,300 as the second of twins. Each person's power is 2.0 kWe to two figures.

### Twins
- **One embryo a pregnancy (agreed with Jeff, 10 Nov 2026).** Every licensed child begins at the clinic, so the clinic decides how many embryos a pregnancy carries, and the rule is one. An allowance counts children, and a second pregnancy set with two embryos would be a third by the back door.
- **That leaves identical twins,** about one pregnancy in 250. Chance sends them. On Earth they were 3–4 births in a thousand in every population, and they hardly ran in families. So no family inherits a way to them, and selection has nothing to work on.
- **A pair at a second birth is a third nobody licensed, and the drum charges it to its room (proposal).** Both children are born to a place. It comes to ~3,300 a year in a nation drum, 3.5% of the room, and it comes off the draw: ~43,600 permits a year where there would have been ~47,000, and a household's chance by the draw alone is about one in eighteen. The second twin is read out on Draw Day with the permits, in the name of a household that ended. A pair at a first birth is the household's two.
- **Conceived the old way, twins are a ratchet.** Fraternal twinning is a matter of how many eggs a mother releases, and it is inherited. On Earth a woman whose mother or sister had fraternal twins had about twice the usual chance; pedigrees put the heritability of the liability at 8–20% for twins of both kinds together, so somewhat more for fraternal alone; rates ran from 2 to 20 per 1,000 across populations; and triplets, mostly from three eggs, sat further out on the same trait at about one birth in 8,000. Where a twin at a second birth is a third the drum accepts, such mothers leave three children where others leave two:

| Heritability of the liability | Twins at 5% of second births | At 12%: all the room | At half |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | ~18,000 years | ~23,000 years | ~31,000 years |
| 0.2 | ~9,000 | ~12,000 | ~16,000 |
| 0.3 | ~6,000 | ~8,000 | ~10,000 |

  (From 1% of pregnancies. The trait shows only in mothers and is carried by both sexes, which halves its speed.)
- **Who that is.** Refuser families and drums, who conceive outside the clinics (people.md § Proofreading), and any drum whose clinics would set two embryos for the asking. Charging twins to the room holds such a drum's numbers only until twins have taken all of it. After that it has to call the second twin born outside, which is a hard thing to tell a mother, or count. A refuser drum where half of second births are twins is somewhere on most rafts.

## The line outside
- **In a fitted drum it is a line of cutters.** A placeless-born couple has a child only by taking out both implants, and the clinic puts them back at the birth and fits the child at fourteen.
- **And they have something to lose.** The placeless-born are in the lot, and one who has a child is out of it, as a household is out of the draw. One chance in seventeen of a place, and two children by right with it, is worth more householders than a child born outside with the same one chance in seventeen.
- **Most have no children anyway.** People want children once they have a home to pass on (people.md), and a lent flat or a house isn't one.
- **So it stays a trace for millions of years.** If one placeless-born couple in 25 cuts, for two children, their line is ~150 births a year in a nation drum, 3.6% of its births outside. The habit is still selected among them, since only the couples who have it are the next generation's parents, and it still leaks into the households through the lot. But the placeless-born are a tenth as many as in a drum that fits nobody and the door is a twentieth as wide, so a quarter of placeless-born couples are willing only after ~2–3 million years, or ~7–11 million from one couple in a hundred (`models/placeless_share.py`).
- **What a fitted drum does about a second cut.** Mostly nothing but refit, and wait. Strict drums fine it or take Hollow hours (rung 2 of people.md's ladder). A line that hides its children from the clinic has made them wild-born, and that is an older and darker argument (people.md § The clinics and the wild-born).
- **In a drum that fits nobody it is the old sore.** There the placeless-born are one person in forty, a child takes no act at all, and nothing is forfeit. Canon takes one such couple in twenty-five having children, three each (proposal). Mixed in with each generation's newly born outside, their children stay a trace for a long time. Kept apart, they don't:

| How the placeless-born pair, in a drum that fits nobody | Births to them, of a drum's births, after 3 / 10 / 20 generations | A tenth of a drum's births in |
|---|---|---|
| At random among all the placeless-born | 0.2% / 0.2% / 0.2% | See below |
| Half the children of the line pair within it | 0.2% / 0.2% / 0.2% | See below |
| The line keeps to itself | 0.2% / 0.2% / 0.2% | See below |
| The same, with the habit passed on strongly (b = 0.5) | 0.3% / 0.4% / 0.6% | ~1,500 years |
| The same, very strongly (b = 0.8) | 0.5% / 2.7% / 38% | ~500 years |
| Old lines marry only old lines | 0.2% / 0.3% / 0.7% | ~1,100 years (~760 at b = 0.5, ~1,800 at b = 0.2) |
| The same, half of each generation placed | 0.1% / 0.1% / 0.1% | Never |
| The same, couples outside held to two children | 0.1% / 0.1% / 0.1% | Never |

  (From `models/placing.py`, which follows the line alone. b is how strongly the habit passes from parents to children: 0.35 unless shown. "Old lines marry only old lines" is the limit in which nothing dilutes a line.)
- **Mixing only buys time.** The table leaves out the door. Everyone placed carries what was selected among the placeless-born back into the households, so the willingness spreads through the whole drum, and each generation's newly born outside arrive with a little more of it. With 5,000 placed a year, a quarter of placeless-born couples are willing after ~60,000–200,000 years, and the drum is a tenth over par ~6,000–27,000 years after that. Once most couples are willing it takes ~70–130 years (`models/placeless_share.py`). The Shade is older than any of those.
- **The threshold.** A line outside grows of itself once the share of its couples who have children, times half the children they have, times the share who aren't placed, passes 1. At three children that is about three couples in four.
- **So shunning makes the caste, and kindness only delays it.** A drum that pushes its placeless-born into quarters of their own has a line within ~500–1,500 years. A drum that keeps them in its houses, halls and crews, marrying whom they like, has one within a few hundred thousand.
- **Earth's line outside drained away,** into towns, armies, colonies and early graves, and still it ran in families: Laslett argued that a few interrelated families lay behind much of an English parish's illegitimacy, generation after generation (others doubt they were so separate), and German guilds asked proof of legitimate birth of an apprentice, of a master and of a master's wife, since her children would carry the lack of it. The Shade has none of Earth's drains.

## What the placeless-born live on
Proposals, from working one such life through (third-chair.md; the sums are `models/placeless_share.py`). Figures are for a fitted drum of 200 M shares. What the same life is in a drum that fits nobody is under Drums that chose otherwise.

### Their poverty does no work
- **The line is drawn in householders, not kWh.** A child outside the allowance costs its household its chance of a third and gains it only the door (Why it holds). Nothing in that sum is a wallet. So how well a drum keeps its placeless-born moves neither its numbers nor the wish.
- **A fitted drum can afford to keep them like anyone.** Its placeless-born are one person in 430. A drum that fits nobody carries ten times as many, and keeps them thinner.
- **One limit.** What a drum gives them can't look like a home to pass on, since that is the cue people have children on (people.md § What births do to the gene pool). A room for life and a waiting wallet both end with the person.

### To eighteen
- **The same childhood.** A placeless-born child has its parents' home, the school, the clinic and the Mill table. What differs is the household's page of the register: a dash beside the name where a brother and sister have *born to a place*, and the note that the household is out of the draw.
- **No keep, and no wallet yet.** A fitted drum has too few to bother posting a price. The child is on its household's wallets, as any child is who was born ahead of the death that will free its share (people.md): five people on four wallets, 17.3 kWh a head a day where a family of four has 21.6. A child born to a place waits about six years for a share. This one waits until eighteen. Where the fault was a clinic's, the drum pays the household a child's wallet until then (third-chair.md): it can make the kWh right, and not the standing.
- **The fitting is the same day for everyone.** At fourteen the whole class goes to the clinic. For most it is the last time the drum treats a child born outside exactly like the rest.

### From eighteen: a waiting wallet
- **About 3% of a drum's shares are unheld at any time.** A share freed by a death waits in its family for the next child born there, and a child born ahead of a death waits for a share. people.md's family-tree model has ~3% of people shareless at par, nearly all of them children, and at par there are as many shares waiting as children: ~6 M in a nation drum, ~5.4 GW of dividends. Left alone, that falls to the commons.
- **A fitted drum lends them (proposal).** From eighteen a placeless-born person holds a waiting wallet: the dividend of a share that is between its death and its child, lent by the drum and moved to another waiting share when the child is born. It is the same 21.6 kWh a day as anyone's, for life. All of them together take 0.36 GW, a fifteenth of the unheld dividends, and no child waits longer for a share.
- **It is a wallet and not a share.** It can't be left, given or named for anyone, and it ends with the person. English dower had the same shape: a widow's life interest in a third of her husband's land, which went back to his heir at her death.
- **A home is a parent's place, a rented room or a house.** A room of 20 m² lets for ~2 kWh a day at par (economy.md § Homes). The parents' place is theirs to live in while the parents live, which for a third child is until about eighty. Then it goes down its own line, to a great-nephew or great-niece in their twenties.

### The implant
- **It stays.** Around thirty a brother and a sister get their places and have theirs out. Someone born outside keeps theirs until the lot places them, at one chance in ~680 a year, or until the entries stop at sixty.
- **Sixty.** Most clinics take it out then for anyone who asks, since nothing rides on it. Some people keep it in, and some wear the scar.

### Old age
- **With a wallet for life, it is nearly anyone's old age.** What is missing is the rent a householder has from the rooms of a place, and the place itself.
- **Kin.** When the parents die their shares go to the family's waiting children and their place to an heir three generations down. A placeless-born elder in a great-niece's back room is the unmarried aunt of every stem family on Earth, with a wallet of her own.
- **Houses.** A house elects its members and gives a room and a hall for life (people.md § The placeless). Housing every placeless-born adult past their twenties would take one place in 560, so in a fitted drum the houses belong to people who chose them, and those born outside are a few among their members.

### Marriage across the line
- **What it costs the partner born to a place.** A marriage across the line brings no children into a place until the partner from outside is placed (Born to a place). A household that has a child outside gives up about an eighth of a householder. Someone born to a place who marries a placeless-born partner and is never drawn gives up two. By the reasoning of Why it holds, selection has made a first marriage across the line rarer than a birth outside, and felt the same way, as a wish and not a law.
- **It waits on the lot.** The partner from outside has their own entries, and the marriage adds none. A couple who marry at twenty-nine have about one chance in twenty-two before the entries stop. A win places the partner, and the implants come out the same week.
- **Marrying late (proposal).** Most marriages across the line are second marriages, to a partner whose children already have households, when they cost a line nothing. Rome's widowers did the same: Marcus Aurelius took a concubine after Faustina died, so as not to put a stepmother over his children, and concubinage was an honourable union with a woman a man couldn't or wouldn't make his wife. Morganatic marriage did it for princes.
- **Widowhood.** The place goes down its own line at the holder's death, and a widow or widower from across the line has no claim on it: a year's grace in most drums, and then lodgings, the house or kin. Where the heir is a child, the trust that holds the place must let its rooms at the going rate.
- **The room it makes.** A couple across the line that is never placed is a household without an heir, and makes room like any other.

### The certificate
- **Standing is settled by birth.** A placeless-born person belongs to the register of the drum they were born in, and so does their claim on a commons. The Glide is free and the next drum's door is 140 seconds away, so without that rule every lenient drum would fill.
- **A drum writes a certificate** for one of its people who lodges elsewhere: it answers for their commons (~1.1 kW, paid to the host) and for any child born to them there, who is the home drum's child and, if it was born outside the home drum's allowance, its placeless-born. England's parishes worked the same way from 1697, and what the certificate replaced was worse: a child born outside marriage was settled wherever it was born, so overseers moved unmarried pregnant women across the parish boundary before the birth.
- **So a glide to a free drum's clinic is a cut by another name.** The clinic there will take out anyone's implants. The child comes home on its parents' certificate, proofread, and placeless-born.
- **A host's door is for its own.** A certificated lodger can work, marry and stay, and isn't in the host's lot. A swap is different: it moves a person's standing with their share and place (people.md § Leaving), and the placeless-born have no place to swap.

### Keepers from outside
- **The heirless have nieces and nephews.** people.md gives the placeless a drum's offices of trust, since nobody without an heir can pass an office on. But a registrar born outside has a brother or sister whose household is in the draw, and a clerk can mend a line in a register. The word nepotism was coined for the nephews of celibate popes.
- **So the draw's keepers come from somewhere else.** Tansy's are seven people from other drums of the Row, sworn for seven years. Italian communes hired their chief magistrate, the podestà, from another city for a year at a time, for the same reason. In a fitted drum the keepers also witness that the clinics take implants out on the Register's word and nobody else's.

## Drums that chose otherwise
About four drums in a hundred don't fit everyone (proposal): they voted the implants out, never kept them after a Count, or were founded by people who left a fitted drum over it. Each is an old answer, worked out in `models/placeless_share.py`, and most of them don't last.

### Free drums: standing alone
- **The rule.** Nobody is fitted. The allowance, the forfeit and the door do all the work (Born to a place). It is what every drum did before its first Count.
- **Where the leak settles.** A free drum's doors are mostly of fixed size: adoption into an heirless place, a crew's berths, a marriage the assembly allows. Births outside adjust to them (Placing):

| Placed a year | What a household forfeits | Births outside | Placed, of those born outside | The placeless-born, of the drum |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | All its leave | 0.5% | 1 in 8 | 0.4% (0.9 M) |
| 2,500 | All its leave | 1.25% | 1 in 8 | 1.1% (2.2 M) |
| 5,000 | All its leave | 2.5% | 1 in 8 | 2.2% (4.4 M) |
| 10,000 | All its leave | 5% | 1 in 8 | 4.2% (8.8 M) |
| 5,000 | The draw only | 4.7% | 1 in 16 | 4.3% (8.9 M) |
| 10,000 | The draw only | 9% | 1 in 15 | 7.8% (17 M) |

  It takes a drum ~7,000–33,000 years to get there with 2,500 or more placed a year, and ~145,000 with 1,000.
- **Canon's free drum** places ~6,000 a year: three births in a hundred are outside the allowance, one in eight of those children is ever placed, and the drum sits 2.6% over par with about one person in forty placeless-born, ~5.3 M of them.
- **What they live on there.** A free drum has too many to lend each a wallet without thinning the commons, and most don't try:
  - **The keep.** The household is charged for the child's keep until eighteen, at a price posted at every station ring (Where drums draw the line):

| The keep | kWh a day | Over eighteen years | The household's wallets, kWh a head a day |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Mill-table credit, 0.23 kW | 5.5 | ~36,000 kWh, 4.6 wallet-years | 16.2 |
| Half the child's commons, 0.55 kW | 13.2 | ~87,000 kWh, 11 wallet-years | 14.6 |
| The whole commons, 1.1 kW | 26.4 | ~174,000 kWh, 22 wallet-years | 12.0 |

  - **No dividend.** The placeless-born stand last in their families' lines for a share. Full-time work pays ~17 kWh a day, four-fifths of a wallet and under half of what a working sibling has.
  - **Old age.** At 120-year lives three people in ten are over 85. With no interest, living on ~10 kWh a day from eighty-five takes a third of a working life's pay, banked from eighteen; stopping at seventy-five takes nearly half. A few free drums lend waiting wallets to those past eighty-five, which takes about a quarter of the waiting shares.
  - **Houses and the box.** Housing every placeless-born adult past their twenties takes about one place in fifty. A house keeps a common fund, as guilds and friendly societies kept a box: fifteen parts in a hundred of pay from election at about thirty to eighty-five pays ~4 kWh a day from then on, beside a room and a hall for life. A monastery's corrody was the same promise.
  - **Houses and par.** Each place a house holds is a household that isn't there, so a free drum can carry its placeless-born as extra people in other households' rooms, 2.6% over par, or house them and have 2% fewer families:

| Houses hold, of the drum's places | Against par |
|---|---|
| None | +2.6% |
| 1% | +1.6% |
| 2.0% (enough to house them all) | +0.6% |
| 3% | −0.4% |

  - **Mortmain with the sums on the board.** Dissolved, such a drum's houses make room for ~3.6 M children at once, forty-one years of leave, and put ~4 M people into lodgings. Every free drum's assembly has heard the motion.
  - **Doors.** Adoption by an heirless household, often of whoever cared for it; long service on a crew that elects whom it takes; and marriage, where the couple enters the draw at a household's odds, about one chance in 450 a year and 6% in thirty years.
- **How it ends.** With the line outside (above). A free drum is steady for tens of thousands of years, and then it isn't: within a century or two of its placeless-born taking to children it is a tenth over par, and it counts. When the Count ends it is a fitted drum, unless it votes the implants out again.

### Marked drums: fitting those born outside
- **The rule.** Households aren't fitted. A child born outside the allowance is, at fourteen, until it is placed.
- **What it does.** It ends the line outside. Births outside still settle by the door, as in a free drum, at seven to fifteen for every placing.
- **What it is.** Counting one group (people.md § Counted drums). The drums that do it say they count nobody, and their neighbours call them marked. In most rafts the clinicians' guild won't fit a child by its standing, so a marked drum trains its own.

### Service drums: children on a wage
- **The rule.** No allowance binds anyone without a share. The shareless pay for the commons they use, and a couple has the children it can pay for.
- **The full commons is out of reach.** Paying for 1.1 kW from eighteen to a hundred and twenty out of sixty-seven working years takes 7.3 kWh an hour, and the hour is ~3.1. So a service drum has a second, thinner tier of commons, down to the survival floor of ~0.5 kWe (people.md):

| Tier | Wage to pay for one's own life, kWh an hour, at 2,000 hours a year | And for 1.13 children each, which is replacement | The same two at 3,000 hours |
|---|---|---|---|
| The full commons, 1.1 kW | 7.3 | 8.8 | 4.9 / 5.9 |
| The lean life, 0.96 kW | 6.4 | 7.7 | 4.3 / 5.1 |
| The survival floor, 0.5 kW | 3.3 | 4.0 | 2.2 / 2.7 |

- **It is Malthus's arithmetic.** At 3.1 kWh an hour and 3,000 hours a year a couple at the floor can pay for four or five children, so the class grows by up to 2.4% a year until its own numbers bring the hour down to ~2.7 kWh. Ricardo called that the natural price of labour: what lets labourers "subsist and to perpetuate their race, without either increase or diminution."
- **Where it settles.** At what the placed pass to the shareless, divided by half a kilowatt each. If the placed spend a quarter of their wallets on other people's time, that is 45 M to 90 M people, 18–31% of the drum. The hull caps it at ~115 M (36%), where the placed have given up all the heat their own wallets made (No rule at all, below).
- **What it is like.** A placed adult buys 440–880 hours of service a year and keeps 20–60% of the private hull heat a wallet runs on. The drum is rich in service and poor in everything else, and it needs a workhouse for those who can't pay.

### No rule at all: the wall
- **What binds first is heat.** A drum's hull radiators are sized for par: 160 GW, 0.80 kW a share (raft-habitat.md). The commons puts ~0.57 kW a head into the hull and a wallet ~0.23 kW, and extra people can't bring radiators with them.

| Placeless | Of the drum | × par | Commons, kW a head | What the placed keep of their private hull heat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 M | 2.4% | 1.02 | 1.07 | 94% |
| 20 M | 9% | 1.10 | 1.00 | 75% |
| 40 M | 17% | 1.20 | 0.92 | 50% |
| 81 M | 29% | 1.41 | 0.78 | None |
| 200 M | 50% | 2.00 | 0.55 | None, and the commons is down to a survival load |

- **How fast.** From a free drum's 4.4 M placeless-born, once their couples have children as readily as anyone: a tenth over par in ~120 years at three children a couple and ~70 at four; the placed's private heat gone in ~240 and ~140; the wall in ~310 and ~180. At the wall the 55-day air reserve is 28 days.
- **It is a stage, and no drum's choice.** A drum gets here when a free drum's line outside takes off, or when an assembly gives rooms the standing of places and looks away. It ends in a Count or in something worse.

### How common (proposal)
- **About three drums in a hundred are free, one is marked, and service drums are one in a few hundred.** Most are among open-drums.md's thin drums, since they sit over par.
- **Every fitted drum has one within a short glide.** A glide metro of ~80 drums holds three or four. They are where a fitted drum's refusers go and where its couples go to have their implants out, and the row's talk about them is half envy.

## How leave is given
Room is one account. Each way of giving it out is a kind of drum, and most drums mix two.

### The draw
- **The rule.** Each year the drum issues as many permits for a third child as it has room it hasn't given another way, and draws them by lot among couples with two children and none outside. A drawn third pays nothing, and the permit takes the couple's implants out again. The same draw places those born outside (Placing). In a holdings drum about half the room goes by naming and half by the draw; in a pool drum the draw is the lottery of unspent birth rights (people.md).
- **The odds.** Over all its years in the draw a couple has about one chance in nine where all the room is drawn, and one in eighteen where the heirless name half of it away. Most never win.
- **What makes it hold.** It is public and fair. The draw runs at every station ring, each permit is named for a household that ended without an heir, and winners often name the child for it, as children elsewhere are named for the dead whose share they carry. Nobody is exempt. A drum with a draw lives or dies by whether people believe the lot.
- **What it selects.** Nothing but entering, and everyone enters. It is the one way of giving leave under which nobody's descendants gain on anybody else's, and the one under which every household, rich or poor, has something to lose by a child outside. That is why most old drums draw.

### Gift
- **The rule.** The heirless give their room to whom they like: in a holdings drum by naming an heir, in a pool drum like Sorrel by giving a birth right to kin or friends (ashgrove.md). Only what nobody gave goes to the draw.
- **What it does.** A third is a relative's or a friend's present, and an heirless great-aunt is the most courted person in a family. It favours the well connected and those who ask hardest, and it puts pressure on the childless (economy.md § Children). Over deep time it selects for tending one's childless kin, which is not the worst thing a rule could breed.

### Sale
- **The rule.** Leave is sold: the drum's half by auction or at a fixed charge, or, in a pool drum like Lovage, birth rights from anyone who will part with theirs (economy.md).
- **What it does.** With room fixed and every couple wanting a third, the price is whatever the richest eighth of couples will pay, and it only rises as they grow richer. Thirds are a rich family's, and "third-born" carries a whiff of money. A household that can't pay has no odds to forfeit, so most cuts are the poor's, and the placeless-born in a drum that sells are a class, if a small one.

### The block's leave
- **The rule.** In open drums the drum's half of the room is the block's to give. A block of ~3,000 has about one third a year, and its court grants it out loud to a couple who asked (open-drums.md § Children without counting).
- **What it does.** Judgment stands where other drums have a lot. The clinic takes the implants out on the block's word. There is no forfeit and no keep. A block that loves a family finds it a third, and may spend its leave on placing one of its own born outside, though seldom for a child whose parents cut, since the neighbours who waited are the court. It works while neighbours know each other well enough to say no, and it selects for standing well with one's neighbours.

### The drum's own children (proposal; rare)
- **The rule.** The childless and the early dead aren't replaced by anyone's third. The clinic draws embryos by lot from every family's banked proofreading samples, and volunteers carry them; the children are raised by their carriers or by tables, and carry the drum's name.
- **What it does.** In a young Shade it was the one rule under which the wish didn't climb, since every family was equally likely to be drawn (The floor under the wish). Now it only takes the gift out of every family's hands. Few drums can stomach it.

## Where drums draw the line
### What takes the implant out (rented rooms; Jeff)
Every holdings drum has to say what a couple in rented rooms, with no place of their own, may do. Drums answer differently (Jeff, 6 Nov): some ban children there, with varying severity; some frown on them, and the children grow up poorer; some don't mind, and those run well above par. In a fitted drum the three rules are three answers to what takes the implants out.

| Rule | What takes the implants out | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Banned (most fitted drums) | A place: the couple's own, by inheritance or by marriage | No child is born in rooms except after a cut, and that child is placeless-born with no later place. Strict drums add penalties for the cut: Hollow hours, deck seniority, posts (rung 2 of people.md's ladder) |
| Frowned on | A wedding between two people born to a place | About one wedding in four comes before the place does (people.md). A child born meanwhile grows up crowded and long shareless, and takes its place when its parents' place comes. Where the place never comes the child stays outside: a small leak that a later place can't mend |
| Allowed | A lease: rooms count as places | Standing has nothing left to withhold. A third born after a cut can rent, marry and raise children of its own, so a cut costs its household only the draw, and cutting is ordinary. Households multiply until every room is let, and the drum runs well over par |

- **What a room drum is like.** Young, crowded, cheerful and loud. Rents run two to four times a steady drum's (economy.md), which works as a price on everyone young, not only on would-be parents; the old who let rooms are rich and the renting young are a class; the commons thins per head; and the drum hits its first Dark harder than its neighbours. It also draws people who want big families from along the row, who swap in, which speeds it up and eases its neighbours.
- **Rent is the only brake it has.** With every couple wanting children and rooms a fixed stock, a room's rent climbs until couples wait for one as they would wait for a place. Where that leaves the drum depends on how many rooms its households will let.

### The keep (the posted price)
- **The rule (proposal; free drums, and a few fitted ones).** A household is charged for the keep of a child born outside its allowance, from the Mill-table credit alone to the child's whole 1.1 kW of commons (economy.md § Children), until the child is grown or placed. The price is posted at every station ring.
- **What it does and doesn't.** It doesn't hold the drum's numbers; the line does that. It keeps the cost of a household's choice off its neighbours, as an English parish bound a child's father to indemnify it for the child's keep. A fitted drum has little to recover, ~0.5 GW of a 220 GW commons, and most post no price. Open drums post none.

### The forfeit and the door
- **The forfeit.** A household with a child outside is out of the draw, and a placeless-born person with a child is out of the lot. Strict drums add penalties: Hollow hours, deck seniority, posts.
- **The door.** In a fitted drum it is the lot, at about one in seventeen. A free drum's doors are as wide as its heirless and its crews make them, and its births outside follow (Drums that chose otherwise).

## Loosening and tightening
- **Every assembly has a party for loosening.** Most households want a third, and a vote can give it to them. In a fitted drum the motions are a third by right, "this once" after a plague or for good; the implants out for a lease; a wider door; and the oldest, that nobody fit the fourteen-year-olds. The cost is spread over the whole drum and lands a generation later.
- **What a third by right does.** With the wish saturated there is no slow climb: three children a couple at once. The drum is 10% over par in ~25 years. At a third over par, in ~50, it is past what its hull can cool at the lean life, and at twice par, in ~95, it is at the wall (No rule at all). Where children still belong to places it levels off ~70% over par, which is already past the first of those (people.md § Counted drums).
- **What voting the implants out does.** Nothing its voters will live to see. The drum is a free drum from that day, and standing holds it: births outside creep up to what its doors allow over thousands of years, and the line outside takes tens of thousands more (Drums that chose otherwise). The motion's backers are right about their own lifetimes, which is why it sometimes carries.
- **Tightening is slow, or hard.** A drum over par by its placeless-born can wait: most of them have no children, so the bulge is gone in a lifetime. A drum that can't wait has to cut births below deaths, and that means an allowance of fewer than two.
- **Standing can ration a third, not a first.** People wait decades for a third and take the lot's answer. Told they may not have a first or a second, an unfitted people has it anyway, and the drum has made most of a generation placeless-born at a stroke, which is no line at all. Württemberg and Bavaria found this in the nineteenth century: laws against the marriages of the poor moved the births outside marriage and stopped few of them (Knodel 1967). So a drum that must shrink leaves the implants in and licenses first and second births by lot: the Count (people.md). A free drum has to fit its people first. A fitted drum needs one vote.
- **The cycle.** A fitted drum votes a third by right and runs thin; a blight or a Dark finds it; it counts for a decade or two; it comes out at two by right again, sometimes with a Margin and open; a few generations on, nobody remembers the Count and the party for loosening wins a vote. Canon's rate is one serious overshoot or shock a drum about every 1,000 years (people.md). England ran one turn of it between the child allowances of the 1790s and the workhouse of 1834.
- **What stays put.** Old drums differ in how leave is given, what takes the implant out, how wide the door is and how much room their clinics leave. None of them differs in whether its people want a third.

## Earth anchors
- **Heirs, not births.** Where land was short, Earth limited who could set up a household and who could inherit, never who could be born (people.md § Places and shares). A child born outside marriage was in English law *filius nullius*, nobody's heir, and still a child of its parish, which fed it and bound it apprentice.
- **No children before a household.** West of a line from St Petersburg to Trieste, Europeans married late and only once they had a living: a farm, a shop, or years of wages saved in service (Hajnal 1965). The fitting does with an implant what that pattern did with custom.
- **Done to every child.** England made smallpox vaccination compulsory for infants in 1853, with fines, and tracked it through the registrars of births. Riots and leagues followed. A conscience clause came in 1898 and was eased in 1907, after which about a quarter of births were exempted (Durbach 2002).
- **Kept after the emergency.** Britain's income tax was brought back in 1842 for three years and has been renewed ever since. The Shade's fitting is a Count's leftover in the same way.
- **The implant by order.** In much of China from the early 1980s the practice was an IUD after a first birth and sterilisation after a second, and a woman couldn't have the device out at will. The Health Ministry's own count in 2013 was 403 million IUD insertions and 196 million sterilisations since 1971. That is the fitting without two by right, which is what the Shade calls a Count.
- **The implant by offer.** From 2009 Colorado's clinics offered long-acting contraception free, and births to teenagers living near them fell by about a fifth (Kelly, Lindo and Packham 2020).
- **A wage that just replaces its earners.** Ricardo's natural price of labour (1817) is the service drum's hour.
- **The keep.** From 1576 English justices could order the parents of a child that fell on the parish to pay for it, and fathers gave bonds to indemnify the parish; from 1610 the mother could be sent to the house of correction for a year. It stayed the law until 1834.
- **A later marriage.** Canon law made a child legitimate when its parents married. At Merton in 1236 England's barons refused to follow it, and English law held out until 1926. Drums split the same way over a later place.
- **Both parents.** From 451/0 BC an Athenian citizen needed two citizen parents, and the *Constitution of the Athenians* gives the reason as the number of citizens. Pericles, who proposed the law, later had to ask the people to enrol his own son by a foreign woman.
- **Service.** Rome's auxiliaries served twenty-five years for citizenship and the right of lawful marriage, which made their children citizens.
- **A line that passes down.** German guilds asked proof of legitimate birth of an apprentice, of a master and of a master's wife. Laslett's "bastardy-prone sub-society" is the argument that illegitimacy ran in a few interrelated families; later work doubts they were a people apart.
- **Moving the births.** Nineteenth-century German laws against the marriages of the poor raised illegitimacy and barely lowered births, and the ratios fell when the laws went (Knodel 1967).
- **Loosening.** Many parishes in southern England paid allowances by the child from the 1790s, births rose where they did (Boyer 1989), and the New Poor Law of 1834 answered with the workhouse.
- **A living for life.** English dower gave a widow a life interest in a third of her husband's land, reverting to his heir. A corrody was a monastery's promise of food, clothing and a room for life. Guilds and friendly societies kept a box their members paid into and drew on. And medieval manor courts recorded agreements by which the old handed a holding to someone, kin or stranger, who would keep them (Clark 1982): the adopted carer.
- **Unions that carried no inheritance.** Roman concubinage and the morganatic marriages of German princes were recognised unions whose partner and children took no rank and no estate (Treggiari 1981).
- **Settlement.** From 1662 an English parish owed relief only to its own settled poor, and from 1697 a person could live in another parish on a certificate by which the home parish promised to take them back. A child born outside marriage was settled where it was born, so parishes harried unmarried pregnant women over their boundaries before the birth.
- **A magistrate from elsewhere.** The podestà of an Italian commune was usually chosen from another city, for a year or less, so that he owed nothing to any local family.
- **Standing by number.** Earth's lines were drawn at marriage. The Shade's is drawn at a number, and the nearest Earth case is recent and coercive: China's children born outside the plan were widely refused household registration, and they were about three in five of the ~13 million people the 2010 census found unregistered, until the State Council ordered everyone registered in 2016. The Shade's line takes less from the child, which is registered, fed and schooled, and in a fitted drum holds a wallet. Whether a line drawn at a number can be as old and as calm as one drawn at marriage was this doc's largest assumption. The fitting is what the Shade has in case it can't.

## How a household passes
Every kind of household below needs an allowance now. The lines on the wish say what each rule did to the climb while there was one (the 6 Nov models); what each rule still decides is who carries a third, and what families are like.

### Whole to one heir (canon)
- **The rule.** A place passes whole to one heir, usually a great-grandchild in their late 20s; the other children marry heirs elsewhere, wait, or stay placeless (people.md). Heirs marry by age and liking, not by a partner's birth order or share.
- **Who carries an extra child.** Where no line is drawn at the birth, everyone a little: a third's chance of a household is every child's chance, so its cost spreads over the whole drum as a slightly longer wait for everyone, a few more placeless and a thinner commons. A share-based marriage market changes nothing, since a third's share deficit lands on some child's wait, not on anyone's marriage. Primogeniture, the heir fixed as the eldest, takes a little off a third's advantage, since the extra child is always a younger one. So canon's kind of household needs the line drawn at the birth: a third born by leave has a place waiting, and a third born without it has none (Born to a place).
- **The wish** climbed at nearly the full speed it would have with a place for every adult; stem places cap a drum's numbers, not the selection.

### Portions (proposal)
- **The rule.** The place still passes whole to one heir. The other children each carry a portion when they marry: a share of the family's savings, belongings and Hollow hours, and, where fixed goods are owned and inherited (economy.md's gentry drums), a claim on rents. An heir marries the best portion they can, and the incoming portion pays out the heir's own siblings, as the Irish farmer's bride's fortune paid his sisters' (Arensberg and Kimball 1940). Three children split what two would have.
- **Family life.** Matches and matchmakers; the portion a public number; marriage late, after the bargaining; a respectable class of unmarried aunts and uncles; the younger children of big families marrying placeless partners or entering houses. "A third is a mortgage on its sisters' matches."
- **The wish.** In the family tree, heirs who chose by portion took a third's advantage from ~0.94 to ~0.85 when three couples in ten wanted a third, and from ~0.82 to ~0.65 when half did. Most households come down the line rule at a death, not through a marriage, so the market reaches only part of who heads one. Where heirs were named young and married strictly by portion, the advantage fell to the floor and the first Count came about two-fifths later than by custom. Portions never held the wish; they decide who pays for a third: the younger children of big families. Today a third born by leave carries a portion like its brothers and sisters, and a child born outside carries none.
- **How it fails.** Portions make wealth tell in every marriage, and a gentry of portions marries itself.

### Split estates (proposal)
- **The rule.** No heir. The place and the family's shares are one estate, split equally among the children as each marries, with a room kept for the old as Central European farmers kept the *Altenteil*. A couple may have children only if their holdings together reach a minimum, as Württemberg and Bavaria made the poor prove their means before marrying in the nineteenth century. The minimum is this kind's line: a child born to a couple under it is placeless-born.
- **Family life.** Small homes; marriages that assemble a holding from two thin ones; equality between brothers and sisters; an early start; no placeless and no heirs. Poverty when the minimum creeps down.
- **The wish.** It climbed anyway. A spouse's holding makes up for a third child's thin one, so the in-laws carry part of the cost, and a split-estate drum holds no fixed number of households for the floor to bind. Over the long run a third's advantage averages a little above the floor while few want one and two to three times it once most do. The numbers swing: a generation born when holdings are thick is followed by a thin one, from a third of par to 1.6× in a model of whole generations (overlapping lives would damp it, and so would couples free to re-match). Requiring each partner, not each couple, to meet the minimum makes both worse. With the wish saturated every holding is split down to the minimum, and the minimum is the whole of the drum's birth policy.
- **How it fails.** Without a law the minimum creeps down, as Ireland's plots did before the Famine. The German laws mostly moved births outside marriage instead of stopping them (Knodel 1967). Split-estate drums are rare and seldom last.

### Sibling households (proposal)
- **The rule.** A place stays with the sibling group for life. Partners visit and never move, as among the Mosuo (Cai Hua 2001) and in the Nayar's matrilineal houses of Kerala. Children belong to their mother's place, or to whichever place the couple names at the birth, and uncles raise their sisters' children. A place is sized for a lineage, a sibling group of two to four in each generation and ~7–14 people at once (three to six times canon's 78 m², and that many fewer places), and when a generation outgrows it the excess leave for houses and Margin flats.
- **Family life.** Big households three generations deep; no wedding ever moves anyone; few couples share a home; every adult is an aunt or an uncle to someone in the next room. Tibet's fraternal polyandry kept holdings whole the same way and left many women unmarried (Goldstein 1976).
- **The wish.** If the excess left by lot, it climbed as fast as by custom; if a mother's third and later children left first, at about two and a half to three times the floor once most couples wanted a third: slower than custom, never still. Today the place's size is the allowance, and where third-born leave first the leavers are known from birth.
- **How it fails.** Over who stays. A household that sends its own away when it overflows has a quarrel in every generation.

## The floor under the wish
Why the Shade is saturated, as the 6 Nov models gave it. The arithmetic is exact for a drum in which a couple who wants a third may have one. An allowance holds the numbers and leaves the argument standing for whoever is given leave.

- **Why there is a floor.** A drum at par replaces each couple with a couple. If one couple in twenty has no children, the couples who have children must leave 2 ÷ 0.95 ≈ 2.1 children who head households of their own, on average. A family of two can leave at most two, so the families with a third must make up the difference: they out-breed families of two by at least 0.105 ÷ their share. Counting the few who die before 35 the room is a little more, ~0.12, which is canon's ~12% of families with a third at par; the models leave the early dead out. It's an accounting identity for any drum whose households are fixed by its places and filled from its own children.
- **What selection sees.** Where some couples who want a third don't get one (a draw, a price), the families who have one still carry the identity, and the advantage of wanting a third is the same room shared among everyone who wants one. So the floor is set by the share who want a third, which is the share who have one only where nothing rations thirds. It assumes couples who don't want a third don't have one, and that childlessness has nothing to do with the wish. The childfree, who want none, are replaced like the infertile, and selection thins them too.
- **What a sibling's cost can and can't do.** A rule that makes a third cost its siblings (a smaller portion, a poorer match, a place that comes later) only moves households between children, since the drum has the same places either way. The room the childless leave still goes to someone's extra child, and only families with extras have one. Lack's principle tunes birds' clutch sizes because an extra chick can starve its brood and fledge none. An extra child in a drum starves no one; it grows up and takes a vacancy.
- **The floor falls as the wish spreads.** At par it is a whole child: every third finds a household. When three couples in ten want a third it is a third of a child, and when most do it is a tenth. Rules that rank children by family size or draw thirds by lot reach the floor by construction, since they place every child of a two-child family first. Rules that leave every child alike let the advantage run near a whole child.

Split estates hold no fixed number of households, so the identity doesn't bind them: their advantage swings from one generation to the next, sometimes below zero, and the figures are long averages with errors of up to ±0.1.
- **Earth runs the same arithmetic.** Where completed family size passes from parents to children, by genes or by upbringing, the lines with more children grow, and fertility rises again after a transition unless new low-fertility ways of living keep arriving faster than selection can catch them (Kolk, Cownden and Enquist 2014; Collins and Page 2019). The drum's models take the parent–child regression of the wish at 0.2–0.5. A Shade with a fast churn of fashions would sit at the low end or below, and everything here would run slower.
- **How fast it went.** From a fresh spread of wishes (`models/ratchet.py`): where custom alone held births, a drum at par reached 10% over and its first Count in ~450–1,000 years, ~300–450 where rented rooms raised children and ~600–1,400 where portions or birth order decided every household. Under a price or a market in rights the numbers held and the strength of the wish climbed a standard deviation every ~800–2,000 years, the price with it. Under a draw half of couples wanted a third after ~1,500–3,800 years and four in five or more after ~10,000. Medicine that left fewer couples childless lowered the floor, to a fifth at one in a hundred, and slowed the last of these. Every road ended in the same place.

## What it feels like
- **Wanting a third is the Shade's commonest ache.** Nearly everyone grew up wanting more children than they would have, and so did their parents. Tables of aunts, fostering and houses are how a drum meets a want it can't grant.
- **The fitting.** At fourteen the class goes to the clinic together, and there is a family dinner after. It is the Shade's commonest rite, and in most drums nobody alive remembers an argument about it.
- **Having them out.** A couple gets its place and goes to the clinic the same week. "They've had theirs out" is how a block hears that a family is starting, and it is told like an engagement.
- **Draw Day.** The year's permits are drawn at every station ring, each read out with the name of the household that ended without an heir. Winners are congratulated like newlyweds. The placings are read last, twenty-eight at a ring. Couples who enter for twenty-five years and never win are a recognised kind of person, and so are the ones who stop entering.
- **The third chair.** A household with a child born outside raised it like the other two and loved it the same. The child knows from the register what it isn't, its brother and sister know what they are, and the parents know what they cut. In a fitted drum most people know one such family, and have an opinion about it.
- **Coming of age.** On the day a brother's dividend goes into his own name, a waiting wallet goes into hers. It is the same 21.6 kWh a day, and it has nobody's name on it.
- **Thirty.** A brother and a sister have theirs out. Hers stays.
- **Marrying late.** A widow, or a man whose children have their places, marries someone born outside, and nothing rides on the lot. It is the commonest way a placeless-born person comes to live in a place, and it ends at a reading.
- **Named for nobody.** Children are named for the dead whose shares they carry, and permits for households that ended. Someone born outside who is never placed leaves neither.
- **Named for a place.** A third born by leave is the heir of a household that ended, carries its name, and grows up visiting the old couple whose home it will have.
- **The courted aunt.** In drums where room goes by gift, the heirless are flattered, visited and fed for forty years, and know why.
- **The price as a class line.** Where leave is sold, thirds cluster among the comfortable, and the cuts are the poor's. People there can tell which third a child is by its family's deck.
- **The match.** In portion drums families bargain, portions are public, and a big family's youngest marries late or enters a house. Gossip is about portions the way other drums gossip about places.
- **Uncles.** In sibling households a child's mother's brothers raise it and its father visits. Inheritance never moves anyone; leaving does, and the leavers are honoured at a farewell meal.
- **Rooms.** In room drums the landlords are old and the tenants are young families; rent is the whole of politics, and the neighbours grumble about the swaps.
- **The free drum down the row.** People in fitted drums talk about it the way people talk about a town with no closing time. They disapprove, and they know the way there.

- **How canon squares Sorrel's ~23,000-year Register with the floor:** answered in Where the Shade is: the wish is saturated in every drum, and most people want a third.
- **How drums with a draw answer thirds born without a permit:** answered in Born to a place: the child is placeless-born, and its household is out of the draw.
- **How many children are born outside the allowance and how many are placed, where nothing but standing holds them:** answered in Drums that chose otherwise § Free drums: births outside settle at seven to fifteen for every placing, whatever the door's size.

## Method
- **The placing model** (`models/placing.py`, output in `models/out/placing.txt`), for a drum with the wish saturated. Room is 1 − (1 − c) × s, with c the share of couples childless (5%) and s survival to 35 (0.993): 5.7% of the births that would refill a drum's households, and a third for 5.7% × 2.01 ÷ 0.95 ≈ 12% of fertile families. *The ledger:* with a leak L (births outside the allowance, per replacement birth) and a door d (the share of them placed), placings are dL, thirds by leave are room − dL, the drum sits (1 − d)L over par and the placeless-born are (1 − d)L ÷ (1 + (1 − d)L) of its people. *Whether a child outside pays:* a household that keeps to its allowance leaves 2 + (its chance of leave) householders, and one with a child outside leaves 2 + d and is out of the draw. Having a child outside is a threshold trait (2% of households by habit and 1% by accident at the start) passed on with regression b = 0.2–0.5, and its spread follows the breeder's equation; a waiting household's chance of leave is the thirds left divided by the households still waiting, so waiting pays more the commoner the habit is, and the same run started at four households in five gives the times for a large leak to mend. *The line outside:* each generation's placeless-born are the newly born outside (trait mean zero) plus the children of the last generation's; one couple in 25 has children at the start, three each; only those couples are the next generation's parents, so their children's mean moves by b × the selection differential. The line is followed mixed in, half apart, wholly apart, and with old lines marrying only old lines, which removes all dilution and bounds how fast a line can take off. Its figures (a 3% leak, one in ten placed) are a free drum's.
- **Where the share settles** (`models/placeless_share.py`, output in `models/out/placeless_share.txt`), per replacement birth, by generations of 33 years. *Free entry:* households have a child outside when a latent habit passes a threshold, and the habit follows the breeder's equation on the gain in householders, (the door's odds) − (the share of leave forfeited) × (a waiting household's chance of leave). With a fixed number A placed a generation the door's odds are A ÷ (births outside), so births outside settle where the gain is zero: A ÷ 0.12 with all leave forfeit, A ÷ ~0.06 with the draw alone. With the door fixed as odds below the forfeit the gain is negative at every size of leak. *The line outside:* a second latent trait, having children while placeless-born; one couple in 25 at the start, three children each; only those couples are parents, so the pool's mean moves by b × the selection differential, diluted by each generation's newly born outside; everyone placed carries the pool's mean into the households (placing.py had no such flow, which is why its mixed lines never grew). *The fitted drum:* births outside 1 in 400, the door 6% as odds, so (1 − 0.06) × 0.25% = 0.23% over par; placings 6% × 4,170 ≈ 250 a year; entered for 42 years at 1 − 0.94^(1/42) ≈ 1 in 680 a year; a line of cutters is the same trait at one couple in 25 or 100 and two or three children, run to 13 Myr. *The wall:* hull radiators 160 GW (raft-habitat.md); the commons' in-hull load 0.57 kW a head and a wallet's 0.23 kW a share, from daily-life.md's budget; a survival hull load of ~0.40 kW a head is an estimate; growth times are ln(target ÷ start) ÷ (ln(k ÷ 2) ÷ 33 yr) for k children a couple. *Service drums:* a life at a commons tier c costs 102 years × c and a child's 18 × c, paid from 67 working years; the class settles at (what the placed pass to the shareless) ÷ 0.5 kW, capped where 0.40 kW of hull heat each uses up the placed's 0.23 kW. *The free drum's sums:* the keep is k × 8,766 h × 18 years, and a household's wallets (4 × 0.9 − k) ÷ 5 kW a head; full-time pay 3.1 kWh × 2,000 h ÷ 365 ≈ 17 kWh a day; old age at no interest, 10 × (years after) ÷ (years worked); the box, 15% × 17 kWh × 54 years ÷ 35 years; houses, the placeless-born past 28 × 35 m² ÷ 78 m² a place. Age shares are from `stem_places.py`'s mortality: 85% of a steady population is over 18, 77% over 28 and 30% over 85. *Waiting shares:* at par shares equal people, so the ~3% of people shareless in `stem_places.py` are matched by as many shares "held for the next child born there": ~6 M × 0.9 kW ≈ 5.4 GW. *Twins:* identical twins at one pregnancy in 250, half of them at a second birth, are 0.2% of births, 3.5% of the room; fraternal twinning as a threshold trait at 1% of pregnancies, a mother with twins at a second birth leaving three children to two, and the liability's mean rising by (h² ÷ 2) × the selection differential a generation, since only mothers show it.
- **The 6 Nov models below** start from a fresh spread of wishes. They give how the Shade came to be saturated, not what a drum looks like now.
- **The floor.** At par the households that have children must average 2 ÷ (1 − c) children who head households, where c is the share childless (~5%). A family of two leaves at most 2, so families with a third average at least 2 + (2 ÷ (1 − c) − 2) ÷ p, with p their share; where thirds are rationed, the same room spread over the share who want one bounds the advantage of wanting one. It holds exactly for a drum whose households are fixed by its places and filled from its own children, where couples who don't want a third never have one and childlessness is independent of the wish. Children who die young or only children raise the floor (to ~0.12 ÷ p with the early dead); households of one would lower it; split estates, which fix no number of households, are outside it. Rules that place every child of a two-child family before any other child reach it exactly.
- **The generation model** (`models/regimes.py`): ~4,000 places, whole generations of ~35 years, a heritable wish for a third (child = mean + b × (midparent − mean) + noise, variance held at 1), a couple having a third when the mean of their wishes passes a threshold set for 10.5% at the start, 5% of couples childless by chance, never more than three children. One regime per inheritance or birth rule; the advantage selection sees is the difference in children who head households between couples who wanted a third and couples who didn't, credited to the generation of those couples. `python3 regimes.py table` prints the advantage by the share wanting a third, mean ± standard error over four runs at b = 0.5 (the table above), and `python3 regimes.py all` the trajectories. Rank, order, the draw and rights to the most eager reach the floor by construction, so the model confirms the arithmetic for them; the family tree is the realistic test of portions. The model's stem places pass to the eldest child. Split-estate drums start with holdings of 0.5 ± 30% of a place each, a minimum of 0.7 of a place a couple, and couples paired once with no re-matching. Sibling households hold 4 or 12 adults a generation; each woman's partner is drawn from the men kept in other places, and every man kept counts as heading a household.
- **The family tree** (`models/share_market.py`, on `models/stem_places.py`): ~15,000 people with 120-year lives and canon's line rule, the wish raised to 2.18 or 2.38 children a couple, every household whose brood is born in a 30-year window followed for 120 years. Four marriage markets: by age (canon), heirs taking only share-holders, like marrying like by share, and heirs choosing the largest portion (1 ÷ the number of siblings).
- **The cohort model** (`models/ratchet.py`): an age-structured drum of 200 M at 120-year lives, with the wish's mean rising by b × the selection differential each cohort; b = 0.2, 0.35 and 0.5; stem and ranked drums settle 1.5× their excess births over par; a Count at 10% over clears to 2% under over ~15 years by lot and pauses selection. Regimes: custom (every child alike), rent pushing back, ranked (the floor, places capping births), rooms (a home for every couple), the draw (the floor, births held at par), a price or market (the most eager tenth pay), the drum's own children (no selection). Its medicine section reruns custom and the draw with 2% and 1% childless, from a wish set for the new par and from one set for canon's 5%.
- **First Counts** quoted are from par with b = 0.5 (fastest) to 0.2 (slowest); the draw's saturation times likewise.
